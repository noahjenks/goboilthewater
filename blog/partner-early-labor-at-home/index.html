What Should the Partner Do During Early Labor at Home?

By Noah Jenks, author of Go Boil the Water  ·  November 6, 2026  ·  6 min read

Quick answer

Keep things calm and ordinary, and save your energy for later. Early labor can take hours or even days, and the NHS says women are usually advised to spend it at home. Your jobs are to keep the house quiet, keep drinks and snacks within reach, rest when she rests, help with breathing or a back rub if she wants it, and keep the midwife updated the way she asked you to.

Key takeaways

  • Early labor, which NICE calls the latent first stage, is the stretch before labor is established, and the NHS says it "can take hours or sometimes days."
  • A partner's best work in early labor is keeping the house calm and ordinary so she can rest and settle in.
  • The NHS suggests that at the beginning of labor a woman can move about, drink fluids, have a snack, try breathing exercises, have a warm bath, and have her birth partner rub her back.
  • Rest when she tells you to, because the night may be long and she will need you awake later.
  • Ask your midwife before labor exactly when and how she wants to hear from you, write it down, and follow it.

In the book I describe two ways the same night can start. In one, a couple rushes to the hospital at the first contractions and gets sent home, more than once. In the other, labor starts at home in the late afternoon. "She goes to lie down." The grandparents play a card game with the kids. "You head to the kitchen, putting a comforting dish on the stove." You text the midwife "to keep her in the loop." Later the doula "suggests you both lie down; it could be a long night."

Nothing dramatic happens in that second version, and that's the point. Early labor at home is mostly waiting, and the partner who handles the waiting well sets up everything that comes after.

What is early labor?

Early labor is the stretch before labor is fully established, when contractions come and go and her body is getting started. The National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) calls it the latent first stage of labor. Its intrapartum care guideline describes it as "a period of time, not necessarily continuous," when there are contractions and some change in the cervix.

The NHS says this phase "can take hours or sometimes days." That's the part to brace for. You don't need to figure out where she is in labor. That's your midwife's job, and she'll tell you what she thinks is going on.

What's the partner's main job in early labor?

Keeping the house calm and ordinary. If she can rest, eat, and settle in, everything goes easier later, for her and for you.

That looks like boring work. Dim the lights. Put on the music she likes. Make something simple to eat. Keep the kids and the phone calls out of her way. One mom told me that when her labor started on her due date, her husband went out "to grab a few things we still needed like popsicles (which I highly recommend having on hand) and some other basics." Errands are fine in early labor. Just keep your phone on and stay close enough to come back fast.

Your mood is the other half of it. In Chapter 8 of the book I wrote that your emotional state is contagious in the birth room. That starts in early labor. If you're pacing the kitchen and checking the clock, she has to manage you on top of everything else. If you're relaxed and a little excited, the house feels that way too.

Should we stay home during early labor?

For a planned home birth you already are home, which is one of the quiet advantages. For hospital births, the NHS says "You'll probably be advised to stay at home during this time," and NICE tells maternity staff to encourage a woman with painful contractions who isn't yet in established labor "to remain at or return home" in most cases.

The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) takes a similar approach. Its committee opinion on limiting intervention in labor recommends that for women in latent labor who aren't admitted, "a process of shared decision making is recommended to create a plan for self-care activities and coping techniques." At a home birth, that plan comes from your midwife. Ask her for it at a prenatal visit.

What should she eat and drink in early labor?

Whatever she feels like, within what your midwife has told you. The NHS says that at the beginning of labor a woman can "drink fluids – you may find sports (isotonic) drinks help keep your energy levels up" and "have a snack, if you feel like it." The World Health Organization's 2018 intrapartum care recommendations say, "For women at low risk, oral fluid and food intake during labour is recommended."

Your job is to keep it close and keep it easy. Don't quiz her. Set the drink next to her and refill it. Nathan, a dad from our survey, said that in early labor "I focused on caring for my wife by bringing her high-protein snacks and making sure she stayed hydrated." That's the whole job description, and it's a good one.

Eat something yourself, too. You'll be no help at three in the morning if you skipped dinner.

Should I sleep during early labor?

If she tells you to, yes. In the book's version of that evening, the doula suggests you both lie down, and later your wife lets you sleep because she knows you'll need it. Take the hint. A rested partner is a better one at the end of a long night.

But rest isn't the same as checking out. Melissa, a mom of four, let her husband Colin rest for a long stretch during her fourth labor so he'd be ready when things picked up. She was so focused that she didn't realize how fast she was progressing, and "by the time I told Colin that I think we might want to call the midwife and let her know things are progressing, within about 15-30 minutes, our baby was crowning." Colin delivered their son before the midwife arrived.

Their story turned out well. Melissa said they "even laughed with each other at how crazy this all was." The lesson for you is simple. Sleep when she tells you to, and keep the midwife updated the way she asked, even if it feels early.

How can I help her through early contractions?

Do the comfort things she wants, at her pace. Under "At the beginning of labour, you can," the NHS lists walking or moving about, drinking fluids, having a snack, trying relaxation and breathing exercises ("your birth partner can help by doing these with you"), having a warm bath, and having "your birth partner rub your back – this can help relieve pain." NICE says "breathing exercises, having a shower or bath, and massage may reduce pain during the latent first stage of labour."

So breathe with her if she wants that. Run the bath. Rub her back. The same mom who sent her husband for popsicles took a walk with him down their steep driveway that morning. If early labor turns into the harder kind, here's how to help during contractions, and what to say to her when it does.

What should I get ready during early labor?

The last few things, quietly. Your supply list should mostly be done by now, and if it isn't, here's how to get the house ready for a home birth. Early labor is the time to make the bed with the layers your midwife asked for, charge your phone, start the playlist, and tell whoever is watching the kids that today might be the day.

If you're planning a water birth, ask your midwife when she wants the pool filled. She'll have a view on the timing. Here's more on the partner's jobs at a water birth.

Keep the guest list where you agreed it would be. In the book I suggest deciding before week 36 who will be in the room and what each person's job is. Early labor is when someone calls asking if they can come over. You get to say not yet.

When should we call the midwife?

When she told you to. Every midwife has her own instructions for when she wants a call, a text, or an emergency call, and they depend on your wife's pregnancy and history. Ask her at a prenatal visit, write down her answer, and stick it on the fridge. If you're unsure in the moment, call. Our midwife, Gloria, told partners to "ask questions if you're concerned in the moment. Not for you to bear the anxiety and fear in silence."

Frequently Asked Questions

How long does early labor last?

It varies a lot. The NHS says the latent phase "can take hours or sometimes days." Your midwife is the one to tell you where she thinks labor is.

What should a partner do during early labor?

Keep the house calm, keep drinks and snacks within reach, rest when she rests, help with breathing or a back rub if she wants it, and keep the midwife updated the way she asked.

When should we call the midwife during early labor?

Ask her before labor starts. Every midwife has her own instructions, so write hers down and follow them, and call if you're unsure.

Can a woman eat during early labor?

NHS advice for the beginning of labor is to drink fluids and have a snack if she feels like it, and WHO recommends food and fluids during labor for women at low risk. Check with your midwife for your own situation.
