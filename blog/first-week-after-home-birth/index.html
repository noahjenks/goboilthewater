What Happens in the First Week After a Home Birth, and What's the Partner's Job?

By Noah Jenks, author of Go Boil the Water  ·  November 9, 2026  ·  6 min read

Quick answer

The midwife comes back to you, and you run the house. After a home birth, your midwife returns to check on your wife and baby, and the World Health Organization recommends that first postnatal contact happen as early as possible and no later than 24 hours after birth. Your job that week is to keep her fed, watered, and resting, handle the visitors so she doesn't have to, take on the baby care you can, and write down what the midwife tells you to watch for.

Key takeaways

  • After a home birth, the midwife usually comes back to the house to check on mom and baby, and WHO recommends the first postnatal contact no later than 24 hours after a home birth.
  • WHO's 2022 postnatal guidance encourages partners to be part of checkups, to support the mother, and to care for the newborn.
  • In the first week a new mother needs food, water, sleep, and someone to manage visitors, and the partner can take on all four.
  • Partners can learn diapers, burping, and settling the baby in the first days so she can rest between feeds.
  • Feeling down or teary for a few days after birth is very common, and the NHS says to get help if it lasts, gets worse, or she is struggling to cope.

In the book I describe the moment most dads aren't ready for. The baby is here, the room has gone quiet, and a couple of hours later the midwife and her team pack their bags, say their quiet goodbyes, and walk out the door. "And it's just you."

At a hospital, somebody hands you discharge papers and tells you when to come back. At home, nobody hands you anything. The first week is yours to run, and most of what happens in it depends on you more than you'd guess.

What happens in the first week after a home birth?

Your wife recovers, the baby learns to feed, and the midwife comes back to check on both of them. As I wrote in Chapter 9, "Your midwife will check in within the first day or two. She'll come back again to monitor your wife's physical recovery, but she's also watching for the emotional and relational signs if something needs attention."

That lines up with what the World Health Organization recommends. In its 2022 postnatal care guidance, WHO says that "In the case of a home birth, the first postnatal contact should occur as early as possible, and no later than 24 hours after birth." For healthy women and newborns, WHO also recommends additional contacts "between 48 and 72 hours, between seven and 14 days, and during week six after birth."

Every midwife sets her own schedule, so ask yours before the birth how often she'll come, how long she'll stay, and how to reach her between visits. Sean, a dad from our survey, described what it looked like for them: "The team came back early the next morning to do some tests etc and check on Emily and the next few days were pretty relaxing with family bringing us prepared food."

"They stayed with us for many hours after the birth, continued checking in on us over the following days, and made sure we had regular follow-up appointments. They were intentional not only about monitoring my wife's physical recovery, but also about checking in on both of us mentally and emotionally."

Nathan, dad

What's the partner's job in the first week?

Running the house so she can recover. In the book I boiled it down to one line: "Practically, in these early days, your wife needs four things: food, water, sleep, and a gatekeeper." Then the next line: "You are the gatekeeper."

Food and water are the easy part, and the easiest to forget. Keep a full water bottle wherever she feeds the baby. Bring her meals without asking what she wants, the same way you did in labor. If friends offer to bring dinner, say yes.

Sleep is harder. She's feeding a newborn around the clock, so her sleep comes in pieces. Your job is to protect every piece. Take the baby after a feed so she can lie down. Keep the house quiet when she's resting. Do the dishes, the laundry, and the dog.

And keep an eye on how depleted she really is. In the book I put it this way: she's "more depleted than she looks and possibly more depleted than she admits."

Should I be at the midwife's postnatal visits?

Yes. WHO's guidance specifically calls for "Encouragement of partner involvement, by being part of checkups, for instance, as well as providing support to the woman and attending to the newborn." You're the one who'll be home when the midwife isn't, so you need to hear what she says.

Keep a running list of questions on your phone between visits. How much is the baby feeding? Is that much sleep normal? Is her bleeding where it should be? You don't need to know the answers. You need to ask them, and write down what the midwife tells you.

How do I handle visitors after a home birth?

Decide before the birth, then say it for her. Some visitors help. In the book I describe the ones who "hold the baby while your wife sleeps, bring a meal and not stay long, read the room and do what's needed. Keep those people."

Others need to be hosted, want to tell their own birth story, and hand the baby back the minute it cries. Those people, "as much as you love them, need to come back in two weeks."

The point is that she shouldn't have to make those calls. "She should not be managing her own social calendar three days after giving birth," I wrote. Set the rules together before labor, then you send the texts. If family is already pushing back on the home birth itself, I wrote about handling family who think home birth is crazy.

What baby care can the partner take on?

Almost everything except feeding, and you can help with that too. Change the diapers. Burp the baby after feeds. Walk the hallway at 2 a.m. with a baby who won't settle. Hand the baby to her and take the baby back.

You'll see things that look alarming and aren't. Sammy, a dad from our survey, said: "nobody told me that babies' poop is black for the first week. The first time I saw it, I thought something was seriously wrong."

It wasn't. The NHS explains that "Your baby's first poo is called meconium. This is sticky and greenish-black," and that "After a few days the poo will change to a yellow or mustard colour." If something does worry you, that's what the midwife's number is for.

Rowan, a dad I interviewed, set up a night system with his wife Genine. When the baby woke, he picked her up and brought her to Genine to feed, then put her back down when she was done. He admits it didn't save Genine many steps. It gave him a real part in those nights from the start. (Here's my full conversation with Rowan.)

What should I watch for in the first week?

Whatever your midwife tells you to watch for, written down. Before she leaves after the birth, she'll go over the signs that mean you should call her right away. Write them on a card, put her number on it, and stick it on the fridge. Don't rely on remembering it at three in the morning.

Watch her mood too. The NHS says: "Feeling down, anxious or irritable for a few days after your baby is born is very common. This is sometimes called the baby blues, and it usually goes away within 2 weeks of the birth. But if it continues, gets worse or you're struggling to cope, it's important to get help." If you notice it lasting, tell the midwife. You see her every day and the midwife doesn't.

And watch yourself. The same NHS page notes that "Fathers and partners can also have depression after having a baby." Sleep when you can. Eat real food. Ask for help when you need it.

What about the first hours?

Protect them. The first stretch after birth is the golden hour, and at home nobody is coming to interrupt it unless you let them. Put your phone down. The world can wait. Here's more on what the golden hour is and how to protect it.

Frequently Asked Questions

How often does a midwife visit after a home birth?

Every midwife sets her own schedule, so ask yours before the birth. WHO recommends that after a home birth the first postnatal contact happen as early as possible and no later than 24 hours after birth, with more contacts in the first six weeks.

What should a dad do in the first week after the baby is born?

Keep her fed, watered, and resting, manage visitors so she doesn't have to, take on diapers, burping, and settling the baby, and be at the midwife's visits with your questions written down.

Is it normal for a newborn's poop to be black?

The NHS says a baby's first poo, called meconium, is sticky and greenish-black, and that it changes to a yellow or mustard colour after a few days. Ask your midwife if anything worries you.

Can partners get depression after a baby is born?

Yes. The NHS says fathers and partners can also have depression after having a baby. Talk to a doctor if you notice it in yourself.
