What Do We Do With Our Other Kids During a Home Birth?

By Noah Jenks, author of Go Boil the Water  ·  November 11, 2026  ·  6 min read

Quick answer

Give them their own grown-up, and don't make it you. Pick one trusted adult whose only job during labor is the kids, plus a backup in case labor starts at 3 a.m. Decide together, before labor, whether the kids will stay in the house, be in the room, or go somewhere else, and ask your midwife how she handles children at births. Then be ready to change the plan if labor moves faster or louder than anyone expected.

Key takeaways

  • Older kids at a home birth need one dedicated adult whose only job during labor is them, and that adult shouldn't be the partner.
  • Have a first choice and a backup for the kids' caregiver, since labor often starts at night and can move fast.
  • Decide before labor whether the kids will stay home, be in the room, or go to a friend or relative, and ask your midwife how she handles children at births.
  • Kids who will be nearby should hear ahead of time that labor is loud and that mom is working, not hurt.
  • A home birth means the kids can meet the baby right away, with no visiting hours, which many families say is one of its best parts.

A lot of couples planning a home birth already have kids. That's part of why home birth appeals to them. Nobody has to drive across town at midnight with a toddler in the back seat, and the kids get to meet their sibling in their own living room.

It also means there are small people in the house while their mom is in labor. Somebody has to feed them, answer their questions, and get them to bed. Here's how to plan for it, from parents who have done it.

Who should watch the kids during a home birth?

Someone who isn't you. Pick one trusted adult whose only job during labor is the kids. A grandparent, an aunt, a close friend, a neighbor your kids know well.

Here's why it can't be you. Sarah, a mom from our survey, said her husband "set up the tub, prayed, took care of our daughter that was in the room watching." He was doing three jobs at once. When I asked whether he showed up the way she needed, she was honest: "At times he was with my 3 yr old daughter and I would have preferred him to be more focused on me." Then, generously, "But I understood the situation."

She shouldn't have had to understand it. You can't hold her through a contraction and pour cereal at the same time. If the kids need you, your wife loses you, so give that job to someone else ahead of time.

What if labor starts in the middle of the night?

Have a backup. Labor doesn't check anyone's calendar, and your first choice may be at work, out of town, or asleep with their phone on silent.

Write down two names. Tell both of them what you're asking, and make sure both have a key or know where it is. If the kids will go to someone else's house, pack their overnight bag at 36 weeks and leave it by the door.

Marissa, a mom whose story is in the book, had a labor that ran three and a half hours from waking up with contractions to holding her baby. Her husband William, she said, "was making them breakfast and actually trying to get them out of the house to run up the road to a friend's house." Fast labors happen. The plan has to work even when there's no time to make one.

Should our kids be in the room for the birth?

That's a decision for you and your wife, made before labor, with your midwife in the conversation. Some families want the kids right there. Some want them asleep, or down the street. There isn't one right answer, but there should be an answer before the day.

Ask your midwife how she handles children at births. Some midwives have a clear view, and she may have practical reasons you haven't thought of. If the kids will be present, the adult watching them needs to be ready to take them out of the room the moment your wife wants space, or the moment the kids want to leave.

Be ready for the plan to change. Marissa was candid: "We thought they would stay and it would be all peaceful and calm, but it was actually quite intense and the kids were getting a bit traumatized." Her kids came out of their room just as she was pushing, and their sister was born. A plan for the kids should include the words "and if it gets intense, they go."

How do we prepare our kids for a home birth?

Tell them what they might hear. Labor is loud, and a child who hears his mom yell without warning may think she's hurt. Explain ahead of time, in words that fit their age, that having a baby is hard work, that mom might make big noises, and that the midwife is there to keep mom and the baby safe.

Give them a job if they want one. Small kids love to help. Picking out the baby's first outfit or bringing mom a cup of water when it's allowed can make them feel part of it. Nick Lampp, a dad I interviewed, said that at their home birth prenatal visits, their little boy even got a turn with the midwife's Doppler. (His story is in my conversation with Nick.)

And tell the kids who will be with them. "When the baby comes, Grandma is going to take care of you" is a lot better than waking up to a stranger.

What did it look like in the book?

In Chapter 4 I walk through how labor can unfold at home, and the kids are part of it. Her parents are in town, and they "settle into a game of Spot It! with the kids in the living room, the familiar noises of family filling the house." There's a family dinner. Then, "The grandparents take the older kids to their home to go to bed. There are hugs, snuggles, giggling about meeting the baby tomorrow. Then the house goes quiet."

That's the picture to aim for. The kids are loved and occupied and out of the way by the time the work gets serious, and you're free to be where your wife needs you. For the rest of that night, here's what a home birth looks like from the partner's side.

What happens when the kids meet the baby?

Often, it's the best part. Marissa said she "loved that we didn't have to keep the older kids away and figure out the logistics of when they could come see the baby. I loved that there were no visiting hours."

Melissa, a mom of four, said much the same thing about the days after: "My husband and kids get to be around as much as they want and life can easily continue on for them in their usual routine."

Plan the meeting with your wife. Some moms want the kids in right away. Others want a quiet hour first, and the golden hour is worth protecting. Either way, let the kids hold the baby while sitting down, with a grown-up close.

What does the partner need to do about the kids after the birth?

Keep their world normal. Older kids may feel shoved aside by a new baby who suddenly gets everything. Breakfast, school runs, bath time, and bedtime stories are yours that first week, so your wife can rest and feed the baby. One mom described her husband "taking care of our kids as well as me and the newborn." That's the job.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can kids be present at a home birth?

Some families have their children present and some don't. Decide together before labor, talk with your midwife about how she handles children at births, and have an adult ready to take the kids out if they or your wife need it.

Who should watch the kids during a home birth?

One trusted adult whose only job is the kids, such as a grandparent or close friend, plus a backup in case labor starts at night. It shouldn't be the partner, who needs to be with the mother.

How do I prepare my toddler for a home birth?

Tell them ahead of time that having a baby is hard work and mom may make loud noises, that the midwife is there to help, and who will be taking care of them while the baby comes.
