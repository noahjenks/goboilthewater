What If I'm Squeamish or Afraid I'll Faint at the Birth?

By Noah Jenks, author of Go Boil the Water  ·  November 13, 2026  ·  6 min read

Quick answer

You can still be a great birth partner. Tell your wife and midwife about it ahead of time, eat and drink through labor, stay up near her head instead of watching the birth itself, and sit down the moment you feel dizzy, hot, or sweaty. The NHS lists not eating or drinking enough and being too hot among the common causes of fainting, and a birth room can be warm and long. If you feel faint, the NHS says to lie down with your legs raised, or sit with your head lowered between your knees.

Key takeaways

  • Many partners worry about blood or fainting at a birth, and saying so to your wife and midwife ahead of time makes it easier to plan around.
  • The NHS lists not eating or drinking enough, being too hot, and standing up too quickly among the common causes of fainting.
  • Partners who eat and drink through a long labor and sit down early are less likely to run out of steam.
  • Your place is usually near her head and face, and you don't have to watch the birth itself to support her.
  • If you feel faint, the NHS says to lie down with your legs raised or sit with your head lowered between your knees, and to see a GP if you do faint.

At our two hospital births, I held Katie's hand and tried not to pass out. I'm not exaggerating much. At Cora's birth the intensity almost threw me into a panic attack, and I spent a lot of that labor just trying to stay upright.

If you're worried about blood, or about hitting the floor while your wife is in labor, you're not the first man to think it. Here's what helps, from dads who've been there and from the NHS.

Is it normal to be afraid I'll faint at the birth?

Yes, and you're not alone. Sammy, a dad from our survey whose wife had complications at their birth, said: "I've never been good with blood or high-stress medical situations." In his full story he went further: "I'm not good with blood or medical situations at the best of times, so mentally I was struggling while trying to stay calm for my wife."

He stayed. That's the part worth noticing. Being squeamish doesn't disqualify you. It's one more thing to plan for, the same way you plan for the birth pool and the drive to the hospital.

Why do people faint?

The NHS fainting page says "There are many reasons why someone might faint," and lists causes that include "standing up too quickly," "not eating or drinking enough," "being too hot," and "being very upset, angry or in severe pain."

Look at that list and then think about a long labor. You've been on your feet for hours. You skipped dinner because it didn't seem important. The room is warm, because the midwife wants it warm for the baby. You're scared for your wife. A man can stack up several of those without noticing.

The good news is that most of them are things you can do something about.

How do I keep from fainting during labor?

Eat and drink. This sounds too simple, and it's the one dads skip most. Nathan, a dad from our survey, went through a labor that lasted more than 36 hours. Looking back, he said, "One thing I would change is making sure I eat more food and maintain my energy throughout the process. I barely ate during the entire labor, and I could feel my strength and stamina drain over time because of it." Todd, a dad I interviewed about his daughter's birth in Bali, forgot to eat too, even though their doula had told them on day one not to. Pack snacks for yourself, not just for her.

Sit down early. If you're kneeling by the birth pool or standing beside the bed for hours, take breaks to sit, and stand up slowly when you do get up. Keep a chair or a stool close.

Cool off. Take a sweater off before you need to. Splash water on your face in the bathroom between contractions.

Know your exit. In the book I give dads one move for when it all hits: "Step out of the room. Not away, just out. Sixty seconds in the hallway, the kitchen, wherever." Breathe slowly, drink some water, and go back in. Here's more on that 60-second reset.

Do I have to watch the baby come out?

No. Your job is her, and her face is at the other end of the bed. Stay up by her head, where she can see you and hear you. Hold her hand. Let the midwife, who has the view and the training, watch the rest.

Some partners want to see their baby born, and some want to help catch. That's great if it's you. If it isn't, nobody is grading you on where you stand. Our midwife, Gloria, told me labor is something men "can't protect their woman from… because they aren't supposed to." Watching her face and believing in her is the job.

Should I tell the midwife I'm squeamish?

Yes, before labor. Bring it up at a prenatal visit, in plain words: "I don't do well with blood. What should I do if I feel faint?" She's heard it before, and she'd much rather plan for it than step over you while she's catching a baby.

Tell your wife too. She may be relieved to hear it ahead of time instead of finding out at the worst moment. Decide together where you'll be when the baby comes, and whether you'll stay at her head no matter what.

What if I start to feel faint during the birth?

Sit or lie down right away, before you fall. The NHS lists warning signs that include "dizziness," "sweating," "feeling warm or hot," "feeling sick," and "changes to your vision." If you feel those coming on, it says to try to "lie down with your legs raised – if you cannot do this, then sit with your head lowered between your knees," and to "drink some water."

The NHS also says that fainting "is not usually a sign of something serious, but if it happens you should see a GP." If you do go down, let someone else handle it, and get checked out afterward.

Then come back when you can. A minute in a chair costs her nothing. Coming back to her side is what counts.

What if something scary happens?

Look at the midwife. The birth of Nathan's son involved serious complications, and he was panicking inside. In the book I wrote that what brought him back "was watching his midwife respond with total confidence and calm." Her steadiness became something he could hold onto.

You don't have to manufacture calm out of nothing. Borrow it from the person in the room who has seen this many times. Frequently Asked Questions

Is it common for partners to faint during birth?

Many partners worry about it. The NHS lists not eating or drinking enough, being too hot, and standing up too quickly among the common causes of fainting, so eating, drinking, cooling off, and sitting down early all help.

What should I do if I feel faint during labor?

Sit or lie down right away. The NHS advises lying down with your legs raised, or sitting with your head lowered between your knees, and drinking some water. If you do faint, see a doctor afterward.

Do I have to watch the birth if I'm squeamish?

No. Stay up near her head, where she can see and hear you. Your job is to support her, and the midwife watches the birth itself.

Should I tell the midwife I'm afraid of blood?

Yes, at a prenatal visit. She can tell you where to stand and what to do if you feel faint, and it's much easier to plan for ahead of time.
