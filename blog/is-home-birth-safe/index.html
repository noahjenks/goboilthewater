Is Home Birth Safe? What Partners Should Know

By Noah Jenks, author of Go Boil the Water  ·  October 7, 2026  ·  4 min read

Quick answer

For healthy women with low-risk pregnancies, planned home birth with a qualified midwife and a hospital transfer plan is generally safe, especially for women who have given birth before. The largest study on the question, Birthplace in England, found no difference in serious outcomes for babies of second-time mothers planning home birth. For first babies the risk was somewhat higher, and roughly 45 percent of first-time mothers transferred to a hospital.

Key takeaways

  • For low-risk women, birth is generally very safe in every setting the big studies looked at.
  • Whether this is her first baby matters a lot. The research is more reassuring for second and later births.
  • Major medical groups disagree on how to weigh the risk. Read both sides.
  • Qualified midwives carry real emergency equipment and screen for risk before and during labor.
  • A transfer plan isn't a backup. It's part of the plan, and you should know it cold.

When Katie first talked about a home birth, my first thought was about safety. Most men's is.

Denny, one of the dads I interviewed for the book, put it plainly: "It was not something I ever wanted nor thought was super safe." Seth said the fear hit once he understood what a home birth really involved. "My wife's protection. My baby's protection."

That's the whole fear. The two people you love most, in a situation you don't understand. I think the fear is worth taking seriously, and the way to take it seriously is to look at what's actually known.

What does the research say about home birth safety?

The largest study on this is the Birthplace in England study, published in the BMJ in 2011. It followed tens of thousands of healthy women with low-risk pregnancies and compared planned births at home, in midwife-led birth centers, and in hospital obstetric units.

A few findings matter most for partners:

  • Serious problems for the baby were rare overall, about 4 in every 1,000 births across all settings.
  • For women having a second or later baby, there was no meaningful difference in serious outcomes between planned home birth and planned hospital birth, and the home births had fewer interventions like C-sections and episiotomies.
  • For women having a first baby, planned home birth carried more risk for the baby: about 9 serious outcomes per 1,000 births, compared with about 5 per 1,000 in hospital.
  • About 45 percent of first-time mothers planning a home birth transferred to a hospital during or right after labor. For second-time mothers, it was 12 percent.

In the United States, the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists takes a more cautious view. ACOG considers hospitals and accredited birth centers the safest settings, and points to US data showing roughly twice the risk of perinatal death with planned home birth, about 1 to 2 deaths per 1,000 births. ACOG also says women have the right to make an informed choice, that a hospital transfer arrangement is a requirement, and that some situations, such as twins, a breech baby, or a previous C-section, rule home birth out.

So the honest answer is that it depends: on whether she's low-risk, whether it's her first baby, how qualified the midwife is, and how close you are to a hospital. Those are things you can find out.

What does a home birth midwife actually bring?

This was the part that changed my mind. My resistance turned out to be less about safety and more about the fact that I wasn't in control and was trusting a system I didn't understand yet. What changed it was sitting across from our midwife and hearing her answer every question without hesitating.

Qualified home birth midwives, such as Certified Professional Midwives and Certified Nurse-Midwives, carry oxygen, IV fluids, medication to control bleeding, and equipment to help a newborn breathe. They screen throughout pregnancy for anything that makes home birth a bad idea, and they watch for warning signs during labor. Nathan, another dad, went in worried about the midwife's medical training. "Clearly I underestimated their knowledge and experience," he said afterward.

One caution: the credentials and licensing rules vary by state. Ask what your midwife's training is and whether she's licensed where you live.

What should I ask the midwife about safety?

Write down every concern before your first meeting and ask them all out loud. A good midwife won't flinch. Three questions to start with:

  1. What would make you recommend a hospital transfer, and how fast can that happen from our house? Get specifics: which hospital, how far, under what conditions.
  2. What emergency equipment do you carry, and what can you handle here? Ask her to show you.
  3. How do you decide whether a pregnancy is right for home birth? How she screens for risk tells you how she manages it.

"It's safe, even if there are 'scary' moments. Ask questions if you're concerned, don't bear the anxiety in silence."

Gloria, midwife

What if I'm still not sure?

That's fine. You don't have to be fully convinced. You do have to be willing to look.

Denny looked. He studied, watched documentaries, talked to couples who had done it, and asked hard questions at every appointment. He called the night his son was born at home one of the greatest of his life. Not every dad ends up there, and some couples look at the evidence and choose a hospital or birth center. Either way, the conversation goes better when your questions come from wanting to understand rather than wanting to win.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is home birth safe for a first baby?

The Birthplace in England study found somewhat higher risk for the baby with a planned home birth for first-time mothers, about 9 serious outcomes per 1,000 compared with 5 per 1,000 in hospital, and a 45 percent transfer rate. Talk this through with your midwife.

Who shouldn't have a home birth?

ACOG lists twins, a breech baby, and a previous C-section as reasons to rule it out. Your midwife will screen for these and other risks throughout pregnancy.

How often do home births transfer to the hospital?

In the Birthplace study, about 45 percent of first-time mothers and 12 percent of women with previous births transferred. Most transfers are not emergencies.

What equipment does a home birth midwife bring?

Qualified midwives typically carry oxygen, IV fluids, medication to control bleeding, and newborn resuscitation equipment. Ask yours to walk you through her kit.
