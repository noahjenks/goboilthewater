My Husband Is Scared of Home Birth. How Do I Talk to Him?

By Noah Jenks, author of Go Boil the Water  ·  November 5, 2026  ·  7 min read

Quick answer

Ask him what he's afraid of, then keep asking. Kristen Nagle, a NICU nurse for 14 years who once believed home birth should be illegal, says a husband's fear usually comes from not knowing. Sit down together, name each fear, look at the facts behind it, and keep asking "what's the worst that can happen?" until you reach the fear underneath. Her own husband went from standing at the head of the bed at their two hospital births to doing counter-pressure through the whole of their third, born at home.

Key takeaways

  • Kristen Nagle worked as a NICU nurse for 14 years and says she was once adamant that home birth should be illegal.
  • At their two hospital births, her husband Chris was, in her words, "a bystander at the head of the bed." At their daughter's home birth he was hands-on the whole way through.
  • Chris took his cue from the calm, quiet women attending the birth, and Kristen says his steadiness helped her feel safe enough to let labor happen.
  • Her approach for a hesitant husband is a conversation: name the fear, ask whether it's really his, look at the facts, and keep going until you reach the root.
  • Asked what makes a great birth partner, Kristen said trust, in each other and in a birth that rarely goes in a straight line.

Kristen and I traded voice messages for a couple of months before we finally found a time to talk. She's a mom of three in Canada, she spent 14 years as a NICU nurse, and she started the Reclaiming Birth conference, which just finished its fourth year.

Her story runs the opposite direction from mine. I came to home birth as the nervous husband. Kristen came to it as a nurse who thought it should be banned. The part I most wanted to hear, though, was about her husband Chris, because he did the thing I'm trying to help every dad do. Here's our full conversation, and below it, what I think every couple with a hesitant partner should take from it.

Who is Kristen Nagle?

Kristen is a former NICU nurse from Ontario, a holistic nutritionist, and the founder of the Reclaiming Birth conference. She and Chris met in Australia, have been together 17 years, and have three kids: two boys, 11 and 8, and a daughter who is 3. The boys were born in the hospital. Their daughter was born at home.

For most of her nursing career, Kristen was where a lot of skeptical husbands are now. "I was adamant that home birth should be illegal," she told me. "If you chose that, you're reckless, you're dangerous, you're putting your baby and your life in danger."

Her thinking changed slowly, over several years and three births. What she said about that is worth hearing if your husband isn't there yet: "You have to be ready to hear these things." She wasn't argued into it. She got there when she was ready, and so did Chris.

How did her husband go from bystander to hands-on?

He stopped watching and started working. At their daughter's birth, Chris spent labor pressing on Kristen's lower back through every contraction.

"He was hands-on. He could not take his hands off my back to counter-pressure. And with the other two births, he was just a bystander at the head of the bed."

Kristen Nagle, former NICU nurse and founder of Reclaiming Birth

"He was fully immersed, fully involved," she said, and when their daughter arrived, it was "We did that." Then she added the line I keep coming back to: "And it was a we."

I know that spot at the head of the bed. At our two hospital births I didn't have a place in the room and didn't know what I was supposed to be doing. If you want the practical side of what Chris was doing, I wrote up how to help during contractions, counter-pressure included.

Why did Chris stay so calm?

He watched the women attending the birth and copied them. Kristen says they were calm, still, and quiet, and at one point there was so little to do that one of them went off to do a Sudoku and take a nap. "Sometimes there's just, you've gotta wait," Kristen said. "There's nothing to do."

Chris read the room. In Kristen's telling, he figured that if they were relaxed enough to nap, "I guess this is normal." So he took on their calm and kept doing the one job in front of him.

That mattered to her more than he probably knew. "That just really helped me feel safe and protected to get out of my neocortex, get out of my head, and allow my body to do what it does best."

Here's what I take from that as a husband. You don't have to manufacture calm out of nothing. You can borrow it from people who have seen hundreds of births. That's one more reason the team you pick matters so much, and why it's worth sitting in on the interview with your midwife together.

What should you say to a husband who's scared of home birth?

Don't argue with him. Ask him. Kristen's advice starts with sitting down together: "Why are you hesitant? Why does this make you nervous? What is coming up for you?" Then, she said, "you have to talk it out."

She walks through each fear the same way. What's the fear, and what's the truth behind it? If something did go wrong, what would you do about it? That, she said, is "just information that you have." For Kristen there's a faith piece too, and then action.

She also asks a question I hadn't thought to ask: "Where's the fear coming from? Is it yours? Is it someone else's?" A lot of what a husband feels is borrowed from his mom, his buddies, or the birth scenes he's seen on TV. If that's where it's coming from, here's how to handle family who think home birth is crazy.

Then there's the exercise she remembers using. "What's the worst that can happen?" You answer. Then you ask it again about that answer, and again, until you reach the fear at the bottom. "Then you face that and be like, 'Is that actually gonna happen? What's the truth behind that?'"

Katie and I do a version of this with every big decision. What's the worst case? Are we still alive? Do we still have our family? Do we still love each other? Usually the answer is yes, and the fear gets a lot smaller once it has a name.

One honest note from me. Some of his fears will be about real risks, and those deserve real answers, not a pep talk. Bring them to your midwife together and ask about her experience, her equipment, and her plan if you need to go in. I've written about what the research says on home birth safety and what a hospital transfer looks like, and neither post pretends home birth is risk-free.

Do hesitant husbands come around?

Some do, and some don't. Kristen has seen both at the conference. Some reluctant husbands came because their wives brought them, and afterward, she said, their attitude was, "Oh, we got this. We can do this." She also hears from women who wanted a home birth and ended up in the hospital because their husband wasn't on board.

Other dads show up already there. Two of them, Simon Esler and Scott Perry, spoke on stage this year. Kristen says they trusted the design of birth, knew their wives could do this, and wanted to find ways to support them through it.

If you're the husband reading this and you're not sure, that's normal. It's where I started. My Wife Wants a Home Birth and I'm Not Sure is the post I wish someone had handed me.

What makes a great birth partner, according to Kristen?

Trust. I asked her for one or two words and that's where she landed. "You have to be on that same page. You have to trust one another." She means trust in each other and in the process, because birth doesn't follow a script. "Birth is not linear," she said.

So the job changes. Sometimes it's counter-pressure for hours. Other times, in her words, "it's just that silent rock that's in the room."

That's the job I didn't understand at our hospital births. Then our youngest, Amani, was born in our bedroom with our midwife and doula there, and for the first time in my life I understood what people mean when they say birth is beautiful. Chapter 2 of the book is written for the husband who isn't there yet.

You can find Kristen on Instagram at @kristen_nagle and learn about the conference at reclaimingbirthconference.com. She's already invited me to the 2027 gathering in San Diego. We'll see where in the world we are by then.

Frequently Asked Questions

What do I do if my husband doesn't want a home birth?

Start with a conversation, not an argument. Kristen Nagle suggests asking him why he's hesitant and what is coming up for him, then going through each fear: where it comes from, what the facts are, and what you would do if it happened. Take the real safety questions to your midwife together.

Why are husbands afraid of home birth?

Mostly fear of the unknown. Many men have only seen birth on TV or in a hospital, and some of their fears are picked up from family and friends. Naming the specific fear, and checking whether it's really his, usually shrinks it.

What makes a good birth partner at a home birth?

Trust, according to Kristen Nagle: being on the same page with her and trusting that birth will unfold in its own way. Sometimes that means hands-on work like counter-pressure, and sometimes it means being a calm, quiet presence in the room.
