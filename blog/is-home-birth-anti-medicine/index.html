Is Home Birth Anti-Medicine?

By Noah Jenks, author of Go Boil the Water  ·  October 19, 2026  ·  5 min read

Quick answer

No. A planned home birth today is attended by a trained midwife who screens for risk, carries emergency equipment, and has a plan for getting to a hospital if one is needed. For most of history babies were born at home, and in the US the move to hospitals happened in about one generation. Families who choose home are choosing where to give birth while keeping medicine close.

Key takeaways

  • For most of human history babies were born at home, and in the US the shift to hospital birth happened mostly between 1940 and 1970.
  • Hospital care brought real lifesaving tools, and nobody planning a home birth today is turning those down.
  • A qualified home birth midwife screens for risk, carries emergency equipment, and has a transfer plan ready.
  • For healthy, low-risk women, especially those who have given birth before, the largest study on the question found planned home birth to be a reasonable choice.
  • If you or your family see home birth as anti-medicine, the midwife's answers to your questions are the best place to start.

A lot of men hear "home birth" and picture somebody turning their back on doctors. Candles and a deep suspicion of anything with a plug.

I had some version of that picture myself. When Katie first talked about having a home birth, my first thought was about safety, and underneath it was a question I didn't say out loud: why would anyone walk away from the hospital?

The answer turned out to be more interesting than I expected, and it starts with how recently the hospital became the normal place to have a baby.

Where were babies born before hospitals?

At home, for almost all of human history. Women gave birth in their own beds, usually with a midwife or experienced women from the community around them.

In the United States that changed fast. According to the CDC's National Center for Health Statistics, 44 percent of births still happened outside a hospital in 1940. By 1969, it was 1 percent.

That's one generation. If you have grandparents or great-grandparents born before the Second World War, there's a fair chance one of them was born at home. The hospital as the only normal option is newer than most of us assume.

That speed matters. When something changes that fast, the new way starts to feel like the only way, and the old way starts to sound reckless. Most people reacting to the words "home birth" are reacting to what has felt normal their whole lives, and that includes a lot of us dads.

Why did birth move to the hospital?

Because medicine got better at things that matter. Over the twentieth century, doctors gained antibiotics, blood transfusions, safer anesthesia, and C-sections that save mothers and babies who would once have died. Those tools lived in hospitals, so birth moved there too.

I'm grateful for every one of those advances. Two of our kids were born in hospitals, and both births went fine.

But something shifted along the way. A system built to handle the hardest births became the default for every birth, including the healthy, low-risk ones. In our two hospital births, I spent most of my time unsure of what was happening or what I was supposed to be doing.

So is choosing home birth anti-medicine?

No. Chapter 1 of the book calls it selective wisdom: using medicine for what it does best, and not using it by default when it isn't needed.

Here's what that looks like in practice. A qualified home birth midwife, such as a Certified Professional Midwife or a Certified Nurse-Midwife, does the prenatal care and screens throughout pregnancy for anything that makes home birth a bad idea. She carries oxygen, IV fluids, medication to control bleeding, and equipment to help a newborn breathe. And every responsible home birth has a plan for getting to a hospital if one is needed. (Here's what that transfer plan looks like.)

Medicine is still in the room, and the hospital is still on the map. It's just not where you start.

Nathan, one of the dads I heard from for the book, went in worried about the midwife's medical training. He came out of it with a different view:

"Clearly I underestimated their knowledge and experience."

Nathan, dad

What does the research say about home birth safety?

For healthy women with low-risk pregnancies, the largest study on the question, Birthplace in England, found no meaningful difference in serious outcomes for babies between planned home and hospital births for women who had given birth before. Those home births also had fewer interventions. For first babies, the risk at home was somewhat higher, and many first-time mothers transferred to a hospital.

In the US, the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists takes a more cautious view and considers hospitals and accredited birth centers the safest settings. ACOG also says women have the right to make an informed choice, and treats a hospital transfer arrangement as a requirement for home birth.

So the medical groups don't all agree, and it's worth reading both sides. I walked through the details in this post on home birth safety. What none of them describe is a choice made against medicine. The debate is about where low-risk birth is safest, with medical care on hand either way.

What if I still feel like it's anti-medicine?

Then take that feeling to the midwife. Ask her what she would do if something went wrong, what she carries, and what would make her call for a transfer. Ask her what training she has and whether she's licensed where you live. Write your questions down before the appointment, because it's easy to forget them once you're sitting across from her. A good midwife will answer every one without flinching.

That's what changed my mind. It was hard to keep thinking of home birth as a rejection of medicine while sitting across from someone who had clearly attended more births than I could count and answered every question without blinking.

If the skeptic is someone in your family, the same approach works. Share the facts calmly, and don't try to win a debate. (I wrote more about handling family who think home birth is crazy.)

Frequently Asked Questions

Is home birth anti-medicine?

No. A planned home birth is attended by a trained midwife who screens for risk, carries emergency equipment, and has a plan to transfer to a hospital if needed.

When did most births in the US move to the hospital?

Mostly between 1940 and 1970. According to the CDC, 44 percent of US births happened outside a hospital in 1940, and by 1969 it was 1 percent.

What medical equipment does a home birth midwife bring?

Qualified home birth midwives typically carry oxygen, IV fluids, medication to control bleeding, and equipment to help a newborn breathe. Ask your midwife exactly what she brings.

Can we have a home birth and still go to the hospital if we need to?

Yes. Every responsible home birth has a transfer plan made with the midwife before labor, including which hospital, how you'd get there, and what would trigger the move.
