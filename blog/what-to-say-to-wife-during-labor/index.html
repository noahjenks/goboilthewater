What Should I Say to My Wife During Labor, and What Should I Avoid Saying?

By Noah Jenks, author of Go Boil the Water  ·  November 2, 2026  ·  5 min read

Quick answer

Say less than you think, and make what you say true. Midwives and moms told me the words that help during labor are short, personal, and spoken close to her, like "you're doing it" or "I'm proud of you." The words that get in the way are questions she has to answer, coaching that belongs to the midwife, and anything that leaves her looking after your nerves.

Key takeaways

  • Many women go deep inside themselves during active labor and talk very little, so a partner's words should be few and easy to take in.
  • Our midwife, Gloria, said the partner often doesn't need to say anything at all, and that being close, with touch and eye contact, says more.
  • Personal, true encouragement lands better than generic lines from a childbirth class.
  • Questions, instructions, and play-by-play commentary usually add to her load, so leave the clinical directions to the midwife.
  • The best time to learn what she wants to hear in labor is weeks before it starts, by asking her and writing down her answers.

At our daughter Cora's birth, in the hospital, I stood next to Katie "holding her hand completely unsure of what I was supposed to be doing or where I should be." I had a vague idea that I was supposed to say encouraging things. I had no idea which ones.

So when I wrote the book, I asked midwives, doulas, and moms what actually helps a woman in labor and what doesn't. I expected a list of good lines. What I got back was mostly advice to talk less.

Should I talk to my wife during labor at all?

Some, but a lot less than you'd expect. Gloria, the midwife at our daughter Amani's birth, has caught more than 500 babies. When I asked her how a husband can be most supportive, she started with this:

"His presence. He doesn't need to say anything in fact it's usually better if he doesn't. The closer he is, physical contact, eye contact."

Gloria, midwife

The moms said the same thing from the other side. Lauralee, a mom of five, told me she communicated in labor "as quickly and as few words as possible." Mikayla, a doula and a mom, said she "usually just said one or two words."

If she's down to one or two words, she doesn't need paragraphs from you. She needs to see you there.

What are the best things to say to her in labor?

Short, true things, said close to her face. Gloria described the partner she loves to see at a birth:

"Face to face and inch or two away (without bad breath), adoring touch- kisses or stroking, adoring words again and again. It's a mantra so it doesn't get old… 'you're amazing. I'm proud of you, you're beautiful, this is amazing, you're doing everything right, wow'"

Gloria, midwife

Notice there's no advice in that list. Nobody's telling her how to breathe. It's a man who is amazed by his wife, saying so, over and over. (Also notice the part about bad breath. Pack mints.)

In the book I put it this way: be the words when she runs out of them. When she's exhausted and starting to doubt herself, your voice matters. Keep it to something like "you're doing it, I can see it, keep going."

What makes some words help and others fall flat?

Whether they're true and they're about her. Mary Beth, our doula at three of our births, told me about one of the best partners she ever watched. "He wasn't repeating generic affirmations from a childbirth class," she said. "He was reminding her of who she was."

You know things about your wife that nobody else in that room knows. You know what she's already fought through and what she's proud of. Remind her of a hard thing she's done before. Tell her you're proud of her. Those lines carry weight coming from you.

It isn't only a feeling that the people beside her matter. The World Health Organization's 2018 intrapartum care recommendations say that "a companion of choice is recommended for all women throughout labour and childbirth." And a 2017 Cochrane review of continuous support during childbirth found that "support from a chosen family member or friend appears to increase women's satisfaction with their experience." The review's authors rated the overall quality of the evidence as low, so read that as a likely benefit, not a promise.

What should I avoid saying during labor?

Anything she has to answer, anything that belongs to the midwife, and anything that makes her take care of you.

Start with questions. Lauralee was blunt about it: "Often times questions felt like burdens. Don't ask if I want water or food- just put it in front of me, if I want it I'll take it." Every "do you want..." makes her climb out of the work to answer you.

Then the coaching. Lines like "push" and "breathe" and "get in this position" belong to the midwife, who can see what's happening. Gloria told me moms can tell when a dad is just filling the air, and that it can leave a mom stalled instead of relaxed. I wrote about that in what midwives wish partners knew.

Last, the words that come from your own nerves. Gloria described what she wants from a partner as "unbroken faith, unwavering presence, not a distracted 'what can I get for you? You ok?'" Asking "are you okay?" in a worried voice hands her your fear to hold. So does commentary on how long it's taking. In the book I list checking the time as one of the signs a man's helplessness is starting to leak into the room.

What if she snaps at me or tells me to stop talking?

Stop talking, stay close, and don't take it personally. Mikayla tells husbands this as a doula:

"She may act differently during birth. She may be loud or yell, she may say things she doesn't normally say. Be there for her. Know she is doing whatever she needs to get through the pain. Don't take offense of any behavior she may direct your way, she's simply coping. Always follow the mom's lead."

Mikayla, doula and mom

Lauralee said there were moments she wanted to tell her husband, "quiet, no touch." That isn't rejection. She needed room to stay with what her body was doing. If she pushes your hand away or tells you to hush, go quiet and stay where she can see you. Offer again later. If it starts to get to you, I wrote about how to stay calm when you feel helpless during labor.

What if the midwife needs her to hear something?

Then your voice can carry the message. Gloria said this happens at a lot of her births:

"Often the dad will be in mom's ear as the midwife is at her pelvis. He repeats and affirms the mom is hearing us when we are trying to get a message through. She listens to him and takes comfort when he says something."

Gloria, midwife

The important word there is "repeats." You aren't adding your own instructions. You're passing along the midwife's words in the voice your wife trusts most. If you're not sure what the midwife wants her to hear, ask her quietly.

How do I find out what she'll want to hear?

Ask her, weeks before labor, and write it down. In Chapter 5 of the book I suggest blocking thirty minutes to ask her what she wants from you. One of the questions is whether she wants you talking to her and coaching her through it, or whether your voice should mostly disappear during active labor.

Here's a version you can do this week. Ask her for three things she'd love to hear from you in labor, and one thing she never wants to hear. Write them on a card. Give a copy to your doula if you have one. Then, on the day, say the three things and drop the one.

Her answers may change once labor starts. That's fine. Follow her lead in the room, and the card still gives you a place to start. (For the rest of the job, here's what a husband actually does during a home birth.)

Frequently Asked Questions

What should a husband say to his wife during labor?

Short, true encouragement spoken close to her, such as "you're doing it" or "I'm proud of you," and reminders of who she is. Many women want very few words during active labor, so less is usually better.

What should you not say to a woman in labor?

Avoid questions she has to answer, coaching instructions that belong to the midwife, comments about how long it's taking, and worried check-ins that leave her managing your nerves.

Is it okay for the partner to stay quiet during labor?

Yes. Our midwife said a partner often doesn't need to say anything, and that staying close, with touch and eye contact, can say more than words.

Does the partner's support during labor make a difference?

The World Health Organization recommends a companion of choice for all women in labor, and a 2017 Cochrane review found that support from a chosen family member or friend appeared to increase women's satisfaction with their birth experience.
