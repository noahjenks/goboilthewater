How Do I Handle Family Who Think Home Birth Is Crazy?

By Noah Jenks, author of Go Boil the Water  ·  October 15, 2026  ·  4 min read

Quick answer

Back your wife clearly and in public, and keep it short. Agree together ahead of time on what you'll say, answer questions with facts about your midwife and transfer plan, and don't debate people who've already made up their minds. Above all, never shrug or quietly side with the skeptic. Your wife will notice.

Key takeaways

  • People will have opinions. Expect it, and decide together how you'll respond.
  • A shrug or "well, it's what she wants" sounds like you don't agree, and she hears that.
  • You don't owe anyone a debate. A calm, short answer is enough.
  • Facts about the midwife and the transfer plan tend to settle the honest worries.
  • Decide early who's welcome at the birth and in the days after, and tell them before the baby comes.

Depending on your family and friends, people are going to have opinions about this. Her mother might forward an article. Someone at dinner will make a comment and then look at you to see how you react.

That look is the moment that matters.

Why does my reaction matter so much?

Because a lot of men fail their wives quietly right here.

They go silent when someone at the table questions the decision. They shrug and say, "well, that's what she wants," in a tone that makes it clear they're not so sure. To her, that shrug sounds like a verdict. Or worse, they privately agree with the skeptic later to avoid conflict, and she finds out.

Staying quiet still picks a side, and she knows which one you picked.

If you still have real doubts, that's fine. Work them out with her, and with your midwife, in private. In public, you're on her side.

What should we say when family pushes back?

Agree on it together before the next family dinner. Something short and calm works better than a defense:

  • "We've looked into it a lot, and we're comfortable with our plan."
  • "Our midwife is licensed and experienced, and we have a hospital transfer plan if we need it."
  • "We'd love your support. We're not looking to debate it."

If someone has an honest worry, the facts usually help. Tell them about the midwife's training, the equipment she carries, and the transfer plan. Plenty of grandparents have never heard that home birth midwives carry oxygen and medication, or that there's a hospital plan in place. Their fear often comes from the same place yours did: not knowing how it works. If they want the research, this post on home birth safety is a good place to send them.

If someone just wants to argue, you don't owe them the argument. Seth went through four home births and was blunt about the outside noise: "Don't listen to others who haven't had a homebirth."

What if her parents are the ones worried?

This one takes more patience, because they love her and they're scared for her. Give them the facts, invite questions, and maybe offer to let them meet the midwife. Then hold the line kindly.

Lauralee said most of her immediate family weren't excited about it and raised their eyebrows, "though kept their opinions to themselves knowing they couldn't change our minds." That's often how it goes. Once family sees the two of you united, most of them settle down.

Who gets to be at the birth?

Decide this early, together, and before anyone asks. Some families want a parent or sibling present, and that can be lovely. But anyone in the room needs to understand their role before they walk in. A well-meaning person who doesn't know their place can disrupt the calm your wife needs to labor well, without realizing it.

If someone is adding stress instead of calm, even with the best intentions, you have both the right and the responsibility to lovingly ask them to step out. This isn't the moment to manage other people's feelings at your wife's expense.

The same goes for the days after. Keep the visitors who bring energy and food, and politely put off the ones who drain it. You're the gatekeeper.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I tell my parents we're having a home birth?

Tell them together, keep it simple, and lead with the facts: a licensed, experienced midwife and a hospital transfer plan. Invite questions without inviting a debate.

What if my mother-in-law wants to be at the birth?

It's your wife's call. If she's welcome, make sure she understands her role and the kind of calm the room needs before labor starts.

Should I argue with people who say home birth is dangerous?

No. Give the facts once if they're open to them. You don't need to win the argument.

What if I agree with some of their concerns?

Talk it through with your wife and your midwife in private. In public, back your wife.
