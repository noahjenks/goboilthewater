How Do I Stay Calm When I Feel Helpless During Labor?

By Noah Jenks, author of Go Boil the Water  ·  October 15, 2026  ·  5 min read

Quick answer

Feeling helpless during labor is normal. You can't take her pain away or speed things up. What you can do is manage your own reaction so it doesn't spill onto her. Know your pattern ahead of time, and when you feel it starting, step back for about a minute, breathe, say something true to yourself, and come back steady.

Key takeaways

  • Helplessness tends to show up right when you most want to feel capable.
  • Men are taught that not being able to fix something means failing. A birth room breaks that rule.
  • Your wife can read your tension even in deep labor, and then she has to manage it.
  • Everyone has a pattern: going quiet, pacing, over-asking, getting frustrated. Learn yours.
  • A short reset, practiced beforehand, keeps the pattern from taking over.

Helplessness doesn't announce itself. It just shows up, usually right when you most need to feel capable, and it brings company: anxiety, frustration, and an overwhelming urge to do something, anything, even if that something makes things worse.

If you're dreading that feeling, you're in good company. One dad in our survey described a frightening moment during his son's birth this way: "I was panicking internally and genuinely feared that I could lose either my wife or our baby." He stayed by his wife's side anyway, and the midwives handled it.

Why does feeling helpless hit men so hard?

From a young age, most of us are told to toughen up, rub some dirt on it, don't show pain. The message underneath is that a man who can't fix the problem in front of him has somehow failed.

That's a bad framework in general. It's a terrible one for standing in a birth room, watching the woman you love go through something you can't control, can't speed up, and can't take from her.

Gloria, our midwife, put it this way:

"This is something they can't protect their woman from, because they aren't supposed to. They have to watch and believe in her rather than protect her from it."

Gloria, midwife

Why does my anxiety matter if she's the one in labor?

Because it doesn't stay yours.

You've felt this before. You walk into a room after an argument and feel the tension before anyone says a word. Or you're around someone so calm that you settle down just by being near them. A birth room works the same way. Even in the deepest part of labor, your wife can read your posture, your jaw, and the way you breathe when you're scared. If what she reads is panic, part of her has to manage you on top of everything else.

What's my pattern?

Think back to a time you felt truly helpless, when the outcome mattered and you couldn't change it. What happened in your body first? Did your chest tighten? Did you go quiet, or start filling the silence? Push harder, shut down, or get angry?

However you handle helplessness in everyday life is how you'll handle it in that room. If your default is to fix, you'll try to fix something that isn't broken. If you withdraw, you'll disappear right when she needs you. If you get frustrated, that frustration will fill the room.

I'll be honest about mine. My helplessness usually turns into anxiety, then frustration. On bad days I have to step away to breathe through it. On better days I catch it early. I'm not perfect at this. As I tell my kids, I'm looking for progress, not perfection.

Those patterns are habits, and once you've named yours, you can interrupt it.

What do I do when I feel it starting?

Have one move ready, and keep it simple. Mine is a reset that takes about sixty seconds:

  1. Step back, or out of the room if you need to.
  2. Breathe slowly a few times.
  3. Say something true to yourself. "She's safe. The midwife has this. My job is to be steady."
  4. Go back in.

That's the whole thing. The men who struggle most are the ones who have no move at all and let the tension build until it's running the show.

Then, when you're back by her side, do the smallest useful thing in front of you. Water. Pressure on her back. A quiet word. Doing something small and steady beats doing something big and anxious every time.

How do I get ready for this before the birth?

Write down your pattern, and be specific. Not "I get frustrated," but something like "when I feel out of control, I start asking unnecessary questions, my jaw tightens, and I pace." The more specific you are, the earlier you'll notice it coming.

Then write down your one move. And practice it once before the birth. The next time you feel that familiar tightening, whether it's stress at work or a hard conversation, run the reset. Breathe, say something true, and come back steady.

The birth room won't give you time to figure this out on the spot, so figure it out now. Chapter 8 of the book goes much deeper on this.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is it normal for dads to feel helpless during labor?

Very normal. Most partners feel it at some point. What matters is having a plan for what you'll do when it hits.

Can my stress affect my wife's labor?

She can read your tension, and managing it takes energy she needs for labor. Gloria has watched labors stall when moms felt micromanaged and couldn't relax.

What should I do if I need a break during labor?

Tell the midwife or doula, step out briefly, reset, and come back. A one-minute break that brings you back steady is better than staying in the room while you're spiraling.

How can I help when there's nothing I can do?

There's almost always a small useful thing: water within reach, pressure on her lower back, the lights dimmed, a few honest words. And your steady presence counts as help.
