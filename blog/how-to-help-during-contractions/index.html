How Can I Help During Contractions? Counter-Pressure, Positions, and Small Jobs

By Noah Jenks, author of Go Boil the Water  ·  November 4, 2026  ·  6 min read

Quick answer

Use your hands more than your words. Before labor, have your doula or midwife show you counter-pressure on her lower back and a hip squeeze. In labor, help her move into whatever position she chooses, keep water and cool cloths within reach, and follow her lead, because what helps during one contraction can be too much during the next.

Key takeaways

  • Moms in our survey named back pressure and hip squeezes as some of the most helpful things their husbands did during contractions.
  • The time to learn counter-pressure is before labor, from your doula or midwife, with your wife telling you harder, softer, higher, or lower.
  • The World Health Organization recommends manual techniques such as massage or warm packs for healthy women who want pain relief in labor, and a Cochrane review found low-quality evidence that massage reduced pain in the first stage.
  • NICE advises helping a woman move and adopt whatever positions she finds most comfortable, except lying flat on her back, so the partner's job is to support her choice, not pick one.
  • What she wants changes as labor goes on, so watch her and let her change her mind.

I learned counter-pressure on the job. Our doula, Mary Beth, coached me through it at Katie's births. As I wrote in the book, "She'd show me right where to press on Katie's lower back to counter the pain, when to lean in close and when to give Katie space." I was grateful every time. I also wished I'd learned it before the night it mattered.

When I asked moms what their husbands did during contractions that helped most, the answers were mostly about hands. Here's what they said, and what the research says about the same things.

What can a partner actually do during contractions?

Mostly, put your hands where she wants them and keep the small things handled. Mikayla, a doula and a mom, described her husband's role in one breathless list:

"He held my hand, squeezed my hips, pressed on my back, kissed me, gave me water, brushed my hair back, held a cool rag to my forehead. I couldn't do it without him!"

Mikayla, doula and mom

None of that requires medical training. All of it requires paying attention. When I asked Mikayla what helped most, she said, "Hip squeezes were amazing!"

What is counter-pressure, and how do I learn it?

Counter-pressure means pressing firmly and steadily into her lower back or hips during a contraction. One mom told me her doula showed her husband pressure points "to help take the edge off the pain." Where to press, how hard, and when are things to learn from your doula or midwife, not from a video the night labor starts.

The moms who answered my questionnaire kept coming back to it. Melissa, a mom of four, said the most helpful thing her husband did was this: "He helped with pushing on my back and hips providing counter pressure during contractions." Sarah wished for "More physical touch like back rubs or hip squeeze." And Lauralee, looking back on her first birth, said "a quick tutorial on how to relieve pressure in hips during contractions would have been helpful for that first go around."

That's the theme. Nobody wished their husband had known less. In the book I put it plainly: "Work with your doula to learn the hip squeezes and counter pressure before labor starts."

At a prenatal visit, ask your doula or midwife to show you both moves. Then practice at home for five minutes while your wife tells you harder, softer, higher, or lower. You're learning her, not a technique.

Does massage actually help with labor pain?

The research says it may, though the evidence isn't strong. The World Health Organization's 2018 intrapartum care recommendations say that "manual techniques, such as massage or application of warm packs, are recommended for healthy pregnant women requesting pain relief during labour, depending on a woman's preferences."

A 2018 Cochrane review of massage and other manual methods for labor pain found "low‐quality evidence that massage provided a greater reduction in pain intensity" than usual care during the first stage of labor, across six trials with 362 women. The authors said the quality of evidence varied "from low to very low."

In the UK, the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) intrapartum care guideline tells maternity staff: "If a woman chooses to use massage techniques in labour that have been taught to birth companions, support her choice." That's you. It only works if someone taught you first.

So keep your expectations honest. Your hands won't take the pain away. They may help, and she'll tell you fast if they don't.

How can I help her change positions?

Follow her body and be something solid to lean on. Picking the position isn't your job. NICE advises maternity staff to "encourage and help the woman to move and adopt whatever positions she finds most comfortable throughout labour, except lying flat on her back." WHO recommends encouraging "mobility and an upright position during labour in women at low risk."

There's some research behind that. A 2013 Cochrane review of positions in the first stage of labor found the first stage "was approximately one hour and 22 minutes shorter for women randomised to upright as opposed to recumbent positions," and women who were upright were less likely to have a caesarean. The authors also said the results "should be interpreted with caution" because the studies varied so much.

Your part is physical. Melissa said her husband, Colin, was there "for me to literally hang on to (around his neck) for different laboring movements." In the book, Sabrina describes her husband James wrapping his arms around her during contractions so she could rest her head on his arm like a pillow. She didn't have to ask. He'd learned what she needed.

Be ready to take her weight, steady her on a birth ball, or walk the hallway with her. If your midwife or doula suggests a change, help her into it. If you're having a water birth, here's what the partner does around the pool.

What are the small jobs between contractions?

Water, cool cloths, and whatever she asked for before labor. Katelyn said of her husband, "He would give me water before I could even tell him I was thirsty." One mom told me that at her first birth she didn't know she'd need "ice to chew on or a fan when I was so hot." Another recommended having popsicles on hand.

Put those things where you can reach them without leaving her. Your supply list should already have most of it, and here's how to get the house ready if you haven't started.

How do I know what she wants in the moment?

Watch her, and let her short answers be enough. One mom described talking to her husband in "Quick phrases like pressure, here, now, harder… he never got frustrated with my shortness but always kept trying to show up how I needed him to."

Some women won't say anything at all. Mikayla told me about her last birth: "he let me bite his hand… haha it's what I needed for some reason and he could just tell."

And what she wants will change. Lauralee said that during transition and pushing, "there was no easing pain with massage or pressure at that point. I just needed a hand to hold(squeeze), someone to tell me I was doing amazing and it would all be over soon." If the hip squeeze stops helping, take her hand. If she pushes your hands away, step back and stay close. For the words that go with your hands, here's what to say to your wife during labor.

Do we need a doula to teach me this?

A doula makes it much easier, especially the first time. Melissa wished she'd known "that having a doula for your first birth is probably a good idea to even help support my husband and to give him live coaching during the labor process." She meant someone "to prompt him of when to offer different types of pressure or massage or help me with position changes."

That's what Mary Beth did for me. She was translating Katie's labor into things I could do with my hands. If you're deciding whether a doula is worth it, here's the difference between a midwife and a doula. If you don't have one, ask your midwife to walk you through the basics at a prenatal visit.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is counter-pressure in labor?

It's firm, steady pressure on her lower back or hips during a contraction, usually from her partner or doula. Learn where and how hard from your doula or midwife before labor, and let her tell you to adjust.

Does massage help during labor?

It may. WHO recommends manual techniques such as massage or warm packs for healthy women who want pain relief in labor, and a 2018 Cochrane review found low-quality evidence that massage reduced pain intensity in the first stage compared with usual care.

What is the best position during labor?

There isn't one best position. NICE advises helping a woman move into whatever positions she finds most comfortable, except lying flat on her back, and a Cochrane review linked upright positions to a shorter first stage.

What if she doesn't want to be touched during contractions?

Stop and stay close. Many women want touch at one point in labor and none at another. Keep water within reach and offer again later.
