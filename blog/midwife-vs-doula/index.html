What's the Difference Between a Midwife and a Doula?

By Noah Jenks, author of Go Boil the Water  ·  October 7, 2026  ·  4 min read

Quick answer

A midwife is a trained medical provider who is responsible for the birth: she monitors mom and baby, manages labor, catches the baby, and handles complications or calls for a transfer. A doula is not a medical provider. She gives continuous hands-on and emotional support during labor, and a good one coaches the partner too.

Key takeaways

  • The midwife is the clinical lead. She makes the medical calls.
  • The doula is the support specialist. She doesn't deliver babies or make clinical decisions.
  • There are different types of midwives (CPM, CNM, CM), and licensing rules vary by state.
  • A doula helps the husband as much as the wife, showing him where to press and when to step back.
  • The partner's role is different from both: he's the person she trusts most, not a medical support.

Before our first home birth I couldn't have told you the difference between a midwife and a doula. I'd have guessed they were two names for the same person. They aren't, and knowing who does what will make your night a lot calmer.

I like to think of it as a team. Every person in a locker room at game time has a job. When everyone knows their position, things run smoothly. When someone wanders in without a defined role, even with good intentions, you get confusion at the worst possible moment.

What does a midwife do at a home birth?

The midwife runs the birth. She's the trained professional responsible for the health of your wife and baby. During pregnancy she does the prenatal care and screens for anything that makes home birth unsafe. During labor she monitors mom and baby, reads how labor is progressing, catches the baby, and handles complications. If something needs a hospital, she's the one who makes that call.

In the US you'll see a few credentials:

  • Certified Professional Midwives (CPM) specialize in out-of-hospital birth. They aren't licensed in every state, so check the laws where you live.
  • Certified Nurse-Midwives (CNM) have a nursing degree plus a master's in nurse-midwifery. They can work in hospitals, birth centers, and homes, can prescribe medication, and are licensed in all 50 states.
  • Certified Midwives (CM) have the same education and pass the same exam as CNMs, coming from a non-nursing background.

The letters matter less than the practical questions. Is she experienced and licensed in your state? Does she carry emergency equipment? Does she have a clear transfer plan?

The night our daughter Amani was born, our midwife Gloria was the calm authority in the room. When labor sped up faster than any of us expected, she took one look at Katie and said, without any panic, "It's time to get in the tub. Your baby is coming fast." A few pushes later, Amani was here. If Gloria had hesitated, we'd have had a baby on the floor.

What does a doula do?

A doula isn't a medical professional. She doesn't deliver the baby and doesn't make clinical decisions. What she does is stay in your wife's corner, and yours, for the entire labor, doing the moment-to-moment work of support.

I think of her as the athletic trainer. She isn't calling plays and she isn't the coach, but without her the players don't stay in the game.

Our doula, Mary, was with us for all three of Katie's births. During contractions she was right there: telling Katie how long each one would last, cueing her breathing, getting her onto the labor ball, guiding her into new positions. And she coached me too. She showed me exactly where to press on Katie's lower back and when to lean in or give Katie space. She was translating what was happening for both of us in real time.

Do we need a doula if we have a midwife?

Not strictly. Plenty of families have a midwife and no doula. But I'd push you to consider one, especially for a first birth, and especially for your sake.

Melissa, who has had four home births, said it better than I can:

"Having a doula for your first birth is probably a good idea to even help support my husband, and to give him live coaching during the labor process: having a doula to prompt him when to offer different types of pressure or massage, or help me with position changes."

Melissa, birth mother

Could we have done it without Mary? Technically, yes. Was every one of those labors calmer and more grounded because she was there? Without question.

So where does the husband fit?

Not as a medical support. That's the midwife's job. Not as the coach either. That's mostly the doula's. You're the relational anchor, the one your wife trusts most and the one whose steadiness she can feel. I wrote more about what that looks like during labor.

Before week 36, sit down together and decide who will be in the room and what each person is there to do. Write it down. Make sure everyone knows before the night arrives, including you.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a doula deliver a baby?

No. A doula provides support, not medical care. The midwife delivers the baby.

Does a midwife replace a doctor at a home birth?

For a low-risk pregnancy, a qualified midwife is the primary care provider. If complications come up, she arranges a transfer to hospital care.

Is a doula worth it for the partner?

Many partners say yes. A good doula shows you how to help in real time, which takes a lot of guesswork out of the night.

What's the difference between a CPM and a CNM?

A CNM is a nurse with a master's in midwifery, licensed in all 50 states. A CPM specializes in out-of-hospital birth and is licensed in some states but not all.
