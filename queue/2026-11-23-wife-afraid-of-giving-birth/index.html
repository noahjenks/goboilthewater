How Do I Help My Wife If She's Afraid of Giving Birth?

By Noah Jenks, author of Go Boil the Water  ·  November 23, 2026  ·  7 min read

Quick answer

Start by asking her what she's afraid of, and listen without fixing it. Help her name each fear and the truth behind it, and take the ones about real risks to your midwife together, so you both hear the answers and the plan. In labor, your job is to be the calm, steady person close to her, saying short, true things like "You're doing it."

Key takeaways

  • Fear of birth is common, and it can show up even in a mom who did every class, book, and breathing exercise.
  • The first job is to ask and listen. A fear she can say out loud to you is easier to work with than one she carries alone.
  • Kristen Nagle, a NICU nurse for 14 years, says to face each fear by asking what it is, what the truth behind it is, and what you'd do if it happened.
  • Fears about real risks belong with your midwife, and the partner should be in the room when she asks them.
  • In labor, a calm and present partner helps her feel safe, and a 2017 Cochrane review found that continuous support in labor was linked to better outcomes, though much of the evidence was rated low quality.

Kelly, a mom from our survey, did everything you're supposed to do before her birth. Classes, breathwork, yoga, meditation, a stack of books. "I logically knew everything I could possibly know," she said. Then labor started, the pain was nothing like she expected, and she told me she "panicked more than I should have."

I like her honesty, because it tells you something before you start. You can't read or plan the fear out of your wife. What you can do is help her carry it, so it doesn't run the room on the day.

This post is for the husband whose wife has said some version of "I'm scared." Maybe it was at the kitchen table, or at 2 a.m., or right after a friend told her a birth story she didn't ask for.

Is it normal for her to be afraid of giving birth?

Yes. Fear of birth is common, and it doesn't mean she's weak or that she's picked the wrong plan. Birth is big and it's unknown, even for women who have done it before.

One mom from our survey said the thing she wished someone had told her husband was "that it could get very complicated and scary." She didn't want him blindsided. She wanted him ready to stand next to her in it.

So don't argue her out of it. "Don't worry, you'll be fine" feels kind when you say it. To her it can sound like you didn't hear her.

What should I do first when she tells me she's scared?

Ask her what she's afraid of, then listen. Don't jump to fixing it.

Kristen Nagle spent 14 years as a NICU nurse and started the Reclaiming Birth conference. When I interviewed her about scared husbands, her advice started with sitting down and asking, "Why are you hesitant? Why does this make you nervous? What is coming up for you?" She was talking about dads, but those questions work just as well when the fear is hers.

She also asks one I'd never thought to ask: "Where's the fear coming from? Is it yours? Is it someone else's? Because a lot of times we're taking on projected fears and bring them onto ourselves."

A lot of what a pregnant woman carries came from somebody else. Her mom's birth. A coworker's horror story. A TV show where someone screams and a doctor runs in. If a fear turns out to be borrowed, that's worth knowing.

How do we talk through a specific fear?

Name it, then look for the truth behind it. Kristen put it this way: "I always say you have to face your fears head on and take them captive. So when you face the fears, okay, what's the fear? What's the truth behind it?"

Then she goes one step further: "if something goes wrong, what do you do about it? that's just information that you have."

Here's how that can look at your table. Write each fear on its own line. Next to it, write what you actually know, and what you'd do if it happened. Some lines you'll be able to fill in yourselves. Others you won't, and that's the point. The empty ones become your list for the midwife.

Kristen also warned about letting fear run unchecked. "The more stories that we play in our head, and that's where we get, that whole idea of what ifs or a slippery slope to despair. So you gotta nip it in the bud." A fear with a name and a plan next to it gets smaller. A fear nobody says out loud grows at night.

Which fears should we take to the midwife?

Every fear about a real risk. Those deserve real answers from the person trained to give them, and a pep talk from me or from you won't cut it.

Go to the visit with her. Bring the list. Ask the midwife what she watches for, what equipment she brings, and what happens if you need to go to the hospital. Then let your wife ask her own questions too. This is one of the biggest reasons for the partner to go to prenatal appointments. You both hear the same answers, and you can remind her of them later.

If her fear is about safety itself, read Is Home Birth Safe? together. It doesn't pretend home birth is risk-free, and she'll trust it more for that.

And if the fear is taking over her days or keeping her from sleeping, tell the midwife now. Don't wait for the next scheduled visit.

What if I'm scared too?

That's normal, and it helps to say so before labor. One dad from our survey told me, "I had reservations but I voiced them all. It was such an unknown world for me I was just scared if an emergency happens what would we do."

He voiced them, and that's the move. Bring your fears to her and to the midwife in the weeks before the birth. Then on the day, she needs your face and voice steady. If you want a plan for your own nerves in the room, read how to stay calm when you feel helpless during labor.

How do I help her feel safe during labor?

Be steady and stay close. That's most of it.

At Kristen's home birth, her husband Chris watched the calm women attending the birth and matched them. It mattered to her more than he probably knew. "That just really helped me feel safe and protected to get out of my neocortex, get out of my head, and allow my body to do what it does best."

"...for her to be free and feel safe in that freedom, there needs to be a very grounded and present Shiva over there... So man is the representation of the groundedness, the presence, and the stability."

Mahika, international doula

Mahika, a doula I interviewed this fall, said that about fathers in the birth room. You don't need to share her spiritual language to get her point. When your wife is scared, you're the thing in the room that doesn't move.

What does that look like with your hands and your mouth? One mom from our survey told me what her husband did: "He would put his head on my head an continually tell me that I could do it and that I was doing a good job. His presence and attentiveness was the most helpful thing."

Short, true, close. Ask her before labor which words she wants to hear when it gets hard, and which ones to never say. I wrote more on what to say to your wife during labor.

Does having support in labor really make a difference?

The research suggests it can. A 2017 Cochrane review of continuous support during childbirth found that women who had continuous support in labor were more likely to have a spontaneous vaginal birth and less likely to have a caesarean, with support from someone like a doula appearing to help most. Support from a chosen family member or friend appeared to increase women's satisfaction. The authors rated much of the evidence as low quality, so read it as a likely benefit, not a promise.

I can't promise you her fear will vanish. I can tell you it changed things for us when I stopped standing in the corner. At our first two births, in the hospital, I didn't know what my job was. When our daughter Amani was born at home, with our midwife and doula there, I knew where to be, and Katie knew I'd be there.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is it normal to be afraid of giving birth?

Yes. Plenty of women feel fear about labor, including moms who have prepared for months. Kelly, a mom from our survey, said she logically knew everything she could possibly know and still panicked more than she expected once labor started.

Should I tell my wife I'm scared too?

Yes, but bring it to the right place. Talk about it with her before labor and take your questions to the midwife together. In labor, keep your face and voice steady and let the team carry the worry.

What do I say when she says she can't do this?

Keep it short and true, and say it close. Lines like "You're doing it" and "I'm right here" work better than long speeches or questions. Ask her before labor which words she wants to hear.

Who should she talk to if the fear won't go away?

Her midwife. If fear about the birth is taking over her days, she should tell her midwife at the next visit or sooner, and you should go with her.
