How Do We Make a Home Birth Plan, and What's the Partner's Part?

By Noah Jenks, author of Go Boil the Water  ·  November 25, 2026  ·  6 min read

Quick answer

Write it together in the last couple of months of pregnancy, keep it to a page, and go over it with your midwife. Cover what she wants the room to feel like, how she wants to be touched and spoken to, the partner's jobs, the hospital transfer plan, and the first hours after birth. In labor, the partner's part is to know the plan well enough to protect it, and to follow the midwife's lead if it has to change.

Key takeaways

  • A home birth plan is a short list of what she wants for labor and birth, written by the couple and reviewed with the midwife.
  • The partner should help write it, because in labor she may not have the words to explain what she wants.
  • Good plans cover the room, comfort and touch, who is there, the partner's jobs, the hospital transfer plan, and the first hours after birth.
  • The transfer plan is the partner's to own: the hospital, the route, a full tank, and the bag by the door.
  • In labor, the partner holds the line on the plan so she doesn't have to, and when the midwife says the plan needs to change, he helps his wife through the change.

Kaelah, a mom from our survey, planned her first birth at a birth center. Her labor started, her water broke, and the midwife sent them to the hospital. Most of what she'd planned didn't happen. What she remembered about her husband was this: "The biggest way that he showed up for me is he fought for me. He made sure that everyone stuck to my Birth plan as much as possible."

He could only do that because he knew the plan. They'd written it together. "Most of all we did a lot of planning and discussing beforehand," she said. "And I'm glad that we did that."

That's what this post is about. A birth plan is a list for her, and it's also a job description for you.

What is a home birth plan?

It's a short written list of what she wants for labor, birth, and the first hours after, made by the two of you and gone over with your midwife. One page is plenty. Many midwives have a form they like couples to use, so ask yours at the next visit.

It isn't a contract, and birth won't follow it line by line. Think of it as the conversation you have before labor, written down so you don't have to have it during labor.

If you're still at the stage of getting the house ready, start with how to get your house ready for a home birth. The plan is the part that lives in your head.

Why should the partner help write it?

Because in labor she may not have the words. Kaelah told me that during contractions "it was really hard for me to talk, especially towards the end because I was so tired." Another mom told me the thing she wished someone had said to her husband was "To anticipate needs I didn't know how to express."

The plan is how you anticipate them. Every line you talk through at the kitchen table is a question you won't have to ask her at hour twelve.

Seth, a dad of four home-born kids, got more deliberate with each birth. "I would journal the good, bad and ugly, debrief and make more notes and have my battle plan in play, not just be on autopilot or wing it." His other piece of advice for couples was "Make sure you both are in total agreement."

What goes in a home birth plan?

Here's what I'd want on the page.

  • The room. Light, music, temperature, and whether she wants the birth pool. Who is there, and who isn't. If you have older kids, who takes them.
  • Touch and words. Where she wants to be touched, and when to stop. Which words help and which ones to never say.
  • Your jobs. Filling the pool, food and water, the phone calls, the door, the dog.
  • The transfer plan. Which hospital, how you'd get there, and what goes in the bag.
  • After the birth. What she wants in the first hour, who gets told and when, and when visitors can come.

Don't skip touch. Kaelah said the one thing she'd have liked more of was physical touch during contractions. "But this was something that I didn't communicate clearly with him." That's a five-minute conversation before labor. Ask her to show you where.

If she wants massage or counter-pressure, get someone to teach you before the day. In the UK, the NICE intrapartum care guideline tells maternity staff: "If a woman chooses to use massage techniques in labour that have been taught to birth companions, support her choice." Ask your midwife or doula to show you, and read how to help during contractions.

Which part of the plan belongs to the partner?

The logistics, and above all the transfer plan. Most dads feel better with a concrete job, and this is a real one.

Mahika, a doula I interviewed this fall, described what a practical dad does to get ready: "He will, have a car nearby, have his tank full, have the address of the hospital nearby. He will prepare the documents." She says that once that's done, a lot of fathers relax.

Todd Parker, whose daughter was born at home in Bali, was blunt about the hospital part. "You can't do an hour if there's an emergency." Your midwife will help you build the plan, including which hospital and when she'd call it. Kaelah said she hadn't looked into hospitals at all before her transfer. "I definitely wish I would have been more prepared and planned for the worst case scenario so that I would have known what I was going into."

Drive the route once. Put the bag by the door at 36 weeks. Save the midwife's number and the hospital's address in your phone. For what a transfer actually looks like, read what happens if you need to transfer to the hospital.

What does the partner do with the plan during labor?

He holds the line on it, so she doesn't have to. One dad from our survey put his advice in one line: "Know the plan and help her stick with it."

Rowan, who caught his daughter in a birth pool in Australia, had a name for it.

"I am the sheriff. I enforce the rules, 'cause we've discussed between me and Genine what the rules are."

Rowan, father of two home-born kids

At a home birth that usually looks small. Keeping the lights low. Telling your mother-in-law the baby will be announced when the baby is announced. Kaelah's husband "made sure to cut anyone off who was trying to talk to me during contractions or bother me." That's the plan at work.

What if the plan has to change?

Then you follow the midwife's lead and help your wife through the change. The midwife is the one who decides when something medical needs to be different. One mom from our survey, whose long labor ended in a transfer, said it well: "a good midwife knows when it's time to go to the hospital for intervention."

Nick Lampp, a birth partner coach, froze at his first son's hospital birth when the staff did the one thing their plan said to avoid. After our conversation, I suggested a question you can always ask: "Is this an emergency, or do we have a minute to talk about it?" If it is, let the team work. If it isn't, you've given her room to decide.

Kaelah's husband did the other half of the job when her plan fell apart. When she cried and told him she didn't think her body could do it, "He supported me and encouraged me. He reminded me that a healthy baby was the main goal."

Write that into your plan too. What matters most to her, in one line, so you can say it back to her if everything else changes.

When the baby is here, the plan keeps going. Read what the golden hour is and how to protect it before you write that part.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do you need a birth plan for a home birth?

You don't have to have one, but most couples find it helps. Writing it makes you talk through what she wants before labor, and it gives the partner something clear to protect when she can't explain it herself. Ask your midwife whether she has a form she likes to use.

What should a home birth plan include?

What she wants the room to feel like, who will be there, how she wants to be touched and spoken to, the partner's jobs, the plan for a hospital transfer, and what she wants in the first hours after the baby arrives.

What does the partner do with the birth plan during labor?

He knows it well enough to protect it without asking her. That can mean keeping the room quiet, handling visitors, and stopping people from talking to her during contractions. If the midwife says the plan needs to change, he helps his wife through the change.

What if the birth doesn't go to plan?

Plans change, and the midwife decides when something medical needs to change. The partner's job is to stay close, keep her informed, and remind her of what matters most to her.
