What Should a Couple Talk About Before a Home Birth?

By Noah Jenks, author of Go Boil the Water  ·  December 2, 2026  ·  4 min read

Quick answer

Before a home birth, talk through why she wants it, what scares each of you, who will be in the room, the rules she wants kept during labor, what happens if you transfer to the hospital, and how the first weeks at home will work. Have these talks over weeks, not in one night, and write down what you both say so the partner can act on it when she can't explain.

Key takeaways

  • Start by asking why she wants a home birth and listening to the whole answer before giving yours.
  • Each partner should name their own fears out loud, and take the ones that need facts to the midwife.
  • Decide together who will be at the birth and who is only called afterward.
  • Her birth rules, written down, give the partner a clear job: keeping them.
  • Talk through Plan B and the first weeks at home while you're both calm.

Rowan, a dad in Australia I interviewed, had a job title at his wife's home birth. "I am the sheriff," he told me. "I enforce the rules, 'cause we've discussed between me and Genine what the rules are."

The second half of that sentence is the important part. He could enforce the rules because they'd talked about them first.

A home birth asks a lot of conversation from a couple. Most of it happens in the months before. Here are the ones I'd make sure you have, and how to have them without it turning into a debate.

Why does she want a home birth?

Ask her, and then listen to the whole answer before you give yours. This is the first conversation and the one most husbands rush. We hear "home birth," something tightens in the chest, and we start building a case.

Don't. Say "tell me more" and mean it. You'll learn what she's hoping for, what she's afraid of, and sometimes what happened at a past birth that she never said out loud. I go deeper on that first talk in my wife wants a home birth and I'm not sure.

What scares each of us?

Both of you get a turn. Her fears matter, and so do yours. One dad from our survey told me, "I had reservations but I voiced them all. It was such an unknown world for me I was just scared if an emergency happens what would we do."

He said them out loud. That's the move. Then sort the fears into two piles. Some need facts, and those go to the midwife at the next visit. Some need comfort, and those are for the two of you. If she's the one who's afraid, read how to help your wife if she's afraid of giving birth.

Who will be in the room?

Decide this together, early, and decide who gets called afterward instead. The midwife and her assistant, maybe a doula, maybe a sister or a friend she trusts. Maybe nobody else.

Then think about the people who'll expect to be there and won't be. Her mom, your mom, the friend who offered to film it. You can tell them now, kindly, or you can tell them at the front door during labor. Now is better. Here's more on handling family who think home birth is crazy.

What are her birth rules?

These are the things she wants kept no matter what: lights low, no phones, no one talking during contractions, no updates to family until the baby's here. Ask her for hers. They'll be different from anyone else's.

Write them down. In labor she won't want to explain them again, and she may not be able to. When the rules are on paper, the partner has a clear job. You keep them. That's what Rowan meant by sheriff.

"Do the research. Do that exploration with your wife. Do it together."

Rowan, father of two home-born kids

What if the plan changes?

Talk about Plan B while you're both calm. Which hospital, how you'd get there, what goes in the bag, who you'd call. Ask the midwife what usually leads her to transfer and how she makes that call.

Kaelah, a mom from our survey who transferred, told me she hadn't looked into hospitals at all beforehand, and wished she had. When the plan has a Plan B written into it, changing course is still part of the plan you made together. Here's what happens if you need to transfer.

What about the weeks after?

Talk about the first weeks home before the baby is in your arms. Who cooks. Who takes nights. How long visitors stay. When you go back to work, and what she needs from you once you do.

This is the conversation couples skip most, because the birth feels like the finish line. Then the midwife packs up and goes home, and the two of you are on your own with a newborn. Better to have the plan before that night.

How do we have these talks without fighting?

Spread them out, one a week. Pick a calm moment, not bedtime after a long day. Let her talk first. Write down both answers side by side, so nobody has to remember who said what.

Katie and I do a version of this with every big decision. It's slower than arguing. It also works.

Frequently Asked Questions

What should we discuss before a home birth?

Why she wants a home birth, each of your fears, who will be in the room, her birth rules for labor, what happens if you transfer to the hospital, and how the first weeks at home will work.

What are birth rules?

Birth rules are the things a mother wants kept during labor, such as low lights, no phones, or no family updates until the baby is born. Writing them down gives the partner a clear job: keeping them.

How do I talk to my wife about my fears about home birth?

Say them out loud, calmly, after you've listened to her reasons. Then take the fears that need facts to the midwife together, and keep talking about the rest between you.
