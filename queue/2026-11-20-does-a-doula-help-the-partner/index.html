Does a Doula Help the Partner Too? What a Doula Does for Dads at a Home Birth

By Noah Jenks, author of Go Boil the Water  ·  November 20, 2026  ·  6 min read

Quick answer

Yes. A good doula supports the partner as well as the mother. Before labor she answers his practical questions and gets him ready, and during labor she coaches him in real time, gives him small jobs, and reminds him to eat and rest. She doesn't replace him. Her work helps him be the steady, familiar person his wife trusts most, a role no one else in the room can fill.

Key takeaways

  • A doula's support covers the father too, and Mahika, an international doula, calls supporting the father "a big part" of the role.
  • A doula is there to help the partner do the job only he can do, which is to be the familiar person his wife trusts most.
  • Before labor, a doula can walk a dad through the practical side of the plan, including the backup plan, which is often what settles his fear.
  • During labor, a doula can coach the partner in real time, give him small jobs, and tell him when to eat or rest.
  • A 2017 Cochrane review found that support from someone there only to provide support, such as a doula, seemed most helpful, though much of the evidence was rated low quality.

At our daughter Cora's birth, in the hospital, I stood next to Katie "holding her hand completely unsure of what I was supposed to be doing or where I should be." What saved me was Mary Beth, our doula. As I wrote in the book, "She'd show me right where to press on Katie's lower back to counter the pain, when to lean in close and when to give Katie space."

I hadn't expected the doula to be there for me. Most dads don't. The question I hear from expecting fathers is some version of "Will a doula replace me?" It usually comes with a little edge, because nobody wants to be the spare guy at his own kid's birth.

If you're still sorting out who does what, start with the difference between a midwife and a doula. This post is about the part of a doula's job that's for you.

Does a doula help the partner too?

Yes, and the good ones count it as part of the job. Mahika, an international doula I interviewed this fall, listed what a doula brings to a birth, and the list ended with you: "Emotional regulation, breath, physiology, postures, and of course, father, supporting the father. That's a big part of this as well."

Mary Beth has worked as a doula for 27 years and now practices as a midwife too. When she described a doula's role at a home birth in the questionnaire I sent for the book, she wrote, "She is also a tremendous support to partners, helping them feel confident and involved in the birth process."

Moms notice it. One mom told me her husband was perfect, then gave credit where she thought it was due: "A large part of that was down to our doula who really managed us both. She was very clear with him what to do. And he's not an egomaniac so just did what she told him."

Will a doula replace me at the birth?

No. A doula is there to help you do the one job nobody else in the room can do. Mary Beth put it plainly when she wrote about partners. "Their role is not to replace the midwife or doula. Their role is to be her person."

"Most importantly, I remind them that no one else can take their place. The doula has a role. The midwife has a role. But the husband or partner brings a unique connection that no one else in the room can provide."

Mary Beth, doula and midwife

Lauralee, a mom of five, showed me how that plays out. At her first birth she leaned hard on her doula early in labor. "Something about being with another woman who has gone through what I was going through gave me strength," she said. Then labor reached its hardest stretch. "During transition and pushing I gravitated to Kaenan because there was nothing left for my doula to do for me physically."

The doula may carry more of the hands-on work for a while. When it counts, your wife will want you.

What does a doula do for the dad before the birth?

She answers his practical questions, which is often where his fear lives. Mahika told me the question about why they'd need her comes especially from dads: "We already have a midwife." With a scared father, she starts with how the home birth is organized and what happens if things don't go as planned. "I understand that the men's psychology works in a very practical way," she said.

Then she said something I may put on a shirt: "This is the psychology of a man who boils the water. What is he gonna do? How he will be able to support? He will have a car nearby, have his tank full, have the address of the hospital nearby. He will prepare the documents."

That's me. Hand me a list and a gas tank and my shoulders come down an inch. Mahika has watched it happen with her clients: "And after that is done, a lot of others already sigh out with the relief."

Nathan, a dad from our survey, had his sister as their doula. "She educated me on every aspect of birth and, most importantly, prepared me for the level of pain and intensity my wife would experience," he said. "Without her, I would not have felt nearly as prepared as I did."

What does a doula do for the dad during labor?

She coaches him in real time and keeps him useful. Mary Beth described what that sounds like from her side of the room: "Sometimes it's simple reminders: 'Stay close.' 'Look her in the eyes.' 'Tell her she's doing well.' 'Help her change positions.' 'Breathe with her.'"

That's what she did for me at Cora's birth. To learn those moves before the day comes, here's how to help during contractions.

A doula also knows a man in that room needs something to do. One dad from our survey spent a lot of his wife's labor filling the birth tub, and he was glad of it: "the team did a great job giving me little jobs to keep me occupied." His wife remembered another moment. At hour 20, "the doula had him try to take a nap." Her verdict: "Spoiler alert: he couldn't sleep."

What does "Do not doula around" mean?

It means your job is presence, and the team has the rest. Mahika always tells fathers the same thing: "Please do not try to be a midwife. Do not try to be a doula. Do not doula around. This is not what we're asking you for. Have a trust in your team and be present."

Then she gives him permission most men won't give themselves: "If you need to self-regulate, if you need to breathe, if you need to nap, if you need to eat, please take care of yourself so that you come back to that center, and go boil the water when we ask you to."

Yes, she said "go boil the water." I didn't pay her to say it.

She has seen what a stressed dad can bring into a room. "There's been numerous situation that woman's birth would start picking up and speeding up when dad would leave the room," she told me. She was quick to add that he wasn't doing anything wrong. "He was stressing out." She was clear about what kind of claim that is: "Of course, it's not a scientific observation. This is just a doula observation."

And she doesn't blame a dad for being scared. "Of course it's scary," she said. "You see the most loved person in absolute agony. Of course it's stressful. It's so understandable, no blame for that." For a plan of your own, I wrote about how to stay calm when you feel helpless during labor.

What does it mean that a father is being born?

It means the birth changes you too, and a doula may be the first person to tell you so. Mahika talks with couples about it before labor. "Not only a baby is being born, a mother is being born," she said. "And then also a father is being born. So technically it's a birth of three." When she says this to a father, she told me, "they're usually very honored and very, inspired by it."

Nobody said anything like that to me before our hospital births. Mahika said it matters for dads to hear birth described as a spiritual event, "because for many fathers, it's the first time that they will hear about it."

Do we need a doula if we already have a midwife?

You don't have to have one, and plenty of families have a homebirth with a midwife and no doula. Gloria, the midwife at our daughter Amani's birth, told me, "The doula can be a fully dedicated comfort specialist for her, and the midwife can help guide and comfort without sacrificing safety or watchfulness."

There's research on the people beside a woman in labor, too. A 2017 Cochrane review of continuous support during childbirth pooled 26 trials with 15,858 women. It found support from someone there only to provide support, such as a doula, seemed most helpful, and that support from a chosen family member or friend appeared to increase women's satisfaction compared with having no companion. The authors rated much of the evidence as low quality, so read that as a likely benefit, not a promise.

If you hire a doula, go to the visits and tell her what scares you. She can't help with a fear she doesn't know about.

Frequently Asked Questions

Will a doula replace the dad at a home birth?

No. A doula supports the mother and the partner, and she helps the partner do the job only he can do, which is to be the familiar person his wife trusts most. Mary Beth, a doula and midwife, tells partners that no one else can take their place.

What does a doula do for the husband?

Before labor, she answers his practical questions about the plan and what to expect. During labor, she coaches him in real time on where to be and what to do, gives him small jobs, and reminds him to eat, rest, and breathe.

Do we need a doula if we already have a midwife?

You don't have to have one. The midwife is responsible for the medical side, and the doula gives continuous support to the mother and partner. A 2017 Cochrane review found support from someone there only to provide support, such as a doula, seemed most helpful, though much of the evidence was rated low quality.

Is it normal for the dad to be scared at the birth?

Yes. Mahika, an international doula, told me, "Of course it's scary," because you're watching the person you love most in pain. A doula can help you prepare for that and find your feet when it hits.
