What Is the Fourth Trimester, and How Does the Partner Help?

By Noah Jenks, author of Go Boil the Water  ·  November 16, 2026  ·  7 min read

Quick answer

The fourth trimester is a common name for the months right after birth, when she's recovering and the baby is adjusting to the world. After the first week, the visitors stop coming and the partner is often back at work, so his job is to keep showing up. Take a regular night or early-morning shift with the baby, do the housework before she has to ask, protect her rest, notice how she's really doing, and go to the postnatal visits.

Key takeaways

  • The fourth trimester is a common name for the months right after birth, when a mother is recovering and her baby is adjusting to life outside the womb.
  • WHO's 2022 postnatal guidance recommends contacts for healthy women and newborns between seven and 14 days and during week six after birth, and encourages partners to be part of checkups.
  • A partner who is back at work can still take a regular night or early-morning shift with the baby so she gets longer stretches of sleep.
  • Doing the housework before she has to ask matters, because asking for every task means she is managing the house and you while she heals.
  • The NHS says to get help if low mood after birth continues, gets worse, or she is struggling to cope, and that partners can have depression after a baby too.

Somewhere around week three, the meal train runs out and your mom flies home. On Monday morning you put on real pants and go back to work.

She doesn't get a Monday. She's still up at 3 a.m. feeding a baby who has no idea what day it is, and she's still healing from the birth. That stretch, after the first week and before life settles into some kind of rhythm, is where a lot of partners quietly drift to the edges. It's also where you can matter most.

What is the fourth trimester?

It's a common name for the months right after birth, when your wife is recovering and your baby is getting used to life outside the womb. Pregnancy has three trimesters, and the months after birth are hard enough, and different enough, that a lot of parents count them as a fourth.

The World Health Organization's 2022 postnatal care guidance is about what it calls the "critical first weeks after childbirth." For healthy women and newborns, WHO recommends postnatal contacts "between 48 and 72 hours, between seven and 14 days, and during week six after birth." The care keeps going after the first week ends, and so does your part in it.

I covered the first days, with the food, the water, the sleep, and the visitors, in what happens in the first week after a home birth. This post picks up after that, when the house is quiet and the help has gone home.

Why can the weeks after the first week feel harder?

Because the help leaves right about when the tiredness has piled up. In the first week, meals arrive and someone is always on the couch ready to hold the baby. A few weeks in, most of that is gone, and she's carrying the same load with fewer hands.

One dad from our survey put it in a sentence: "For us, postpartum was actually harder than the birth itself." Men prepare for the birth like it's the big game. Nobody tells them the season starts after.

When I asked moms what they wished their midwife had done differently to prepare their partner, one mom answered: "Preparing him on how much care a women needs postpartum." Consider this your heads-up. She needs a lot of care, and she needs it for longer than a week.

What does it mean that a father is being born?

It means you're becoming someone new in these months too. Mahika, an international doula I interviewed, says this to the dads she works with. "Because not only a baby is being born, a mother is being born," she told me. Then she added the part for us.

"And then also a father is being born. So technically it's a birth of three, at least, of three in that birth."

Mahika, international doula

She said that when she tells fathers this, "they're usually very honored and very, inspired by it." I believe it. The birth makes you a dad on paper. The fourth trimester is where you learn how to be one, a diaper and a bad night at a time.

How do I help with nights when I'm back at work?

Take a shift you can keep, and keep it. Nights are where the fourth trimester wears a couple down, and they're also where a working dad can give her something nobody else can.

One mom described what her husband did, and I'd hand this to every new dad if I could: "He even would take shifts with the new baby so I could sleep longer and eventually giving the baby a bottle as well as being in charge of all night time diaper changing and burping to again allow me as much rest as possible." And later: "as baby would grow, he would be a pivotal bottle giver early hours of the morning before he would go into work giving me more sleep."

Notice what he didn't do. He didn't wait for her to wake him up and ask. He owned the night diapers and the burping, and he took the early morning before work. Sit down with your wife and agree on a plan for nights. Which stretch is yours, and does it change on a work night versus a weekend? How the baby is fed is her call and your midwife's, so build your shifts around that plan, whatever it is.

Your shift will cost you sleep. That's the trade. Go to bed early on your off nights, and say no to anything that can wait a few months.

What housework should I do without being asked?

Anything she would otherwise have to ask you for. The asking is the problem. In my post on the golden hour and the days after it, I wrote that when she has to ask for every glass of water or remind you that the dishes need doing, "she's managing herself and you, and over a few weeks that wears her down." The fourth trimester is those few weeks.

So look around the house the way she would. The dishwasher is clean, so empty it. The laundry basket is overflowing. The fridge is down to condiments and half a lemon. The trash goes out Thursday. None of this is heroic. Left undone, all of it lands on a woman who is healing and feeding a baby around the clock.

If you have older kids, take as much of them as you can. School runs and the bedtime routine give her hours back.

How do I protect her rest once the visitors stop coming?

Keep being the gatekeeper, even when there's nobody at the gate. In the first week, the threat to her rest is the doorbell. Later it's quieter. It's the group chat, the relative who wants to stop by for just a minute, and her own list of things she thinks she should be getting done.

Give her real time off. When you get home from work, take the baby before you look at your phone. On a weekend morning, take the baby out for a walk so she can sleep in a quiet house. If she wants a long shower, stand guard. Tell her she's off duty, and mean it.

How do I notice how she's really doing?

Ask her, then watch. You see her every day, and her midwife doesn't. She may tell you she's fine because the baby is fed and the house is still standing, so pay attention to more than the answer.

Ask specific questions, like how she slept and what would make tomorrow easier. Then listen without trying to fix it.

The NHS says: "Feeling down, anxious or irritable for a few days after your baby is born is very common. This is sometimes called the baby blues, and it usually goes away within 2 weeks of the birth. But if it continues, gets worse or you're struggling to cope, it's important to get help." If you notice her struggling, tell her midwife or doctor. The same NHS page notes that "Fathers and partners can also have depression after having a baby," so keep an eye on yourself too.

Should I go to the postnatal visits after the first week?

Yes, every one you can. WHO's guidance calls for "Encouragement of partner involvement, by being part of checkups, for instance, as well as providing support to the woman and attending to the newborn." That doesn't end on day seven.

Ask your midwife before the birth how many visits she does after it and roughly when, then block them on your work calendar now. If you went to the prenatal appointments, you already know why. You hear what the midwife says firsthand, and your wife doesn't have to remember it all and repeat it to you that night.

Bring your list of questions. By week six you'll have a few you didn't know to ask in week one.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the fourth trimester?

It's a common name for the months right after birth, when a mother is recovering and her baby is adjusting to life outside the womb. For the partner, it's the stretch after the first week, when the visitors thin out and he's often back at work.

How can a dad help at night if he's back at work?

Agree on a regular shift he can keep, such as the night diapers and burping or the early-morning stretch before work, so she gets longer blocks of sleep. Build it around the feeding plan she and your midwife have made.

Should the partner go to postnatal checkups?

Yes, whenever he can. WHO's 2022 postnatal guidance encourages partners to be part of checkups. Ask your midwife early how many visits she does so you can plan time off work.

What should I do if my wife doesn't seem like herself weeks after the birth?

Tell her midwife or doctor. The NHS says that if low mood after birth continues, gets worse, or she is struggling to cope, it's important to get help.
