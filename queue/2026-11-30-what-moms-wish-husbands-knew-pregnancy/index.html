What Do Moms Wish Their Husbands Knew During Pregnancy?

By Noah Jenks, author of Go Boil the Water  ·  November 30, 2026  ·  5 min read

Quick answer

Moms in our survey wished their husbands knew that the birth starts long before labor. They wanted him at the prenatal visits, learning ahead of time what she'll need, because in labor she may not have the words. They wanted him ready for a long or complicated labor, and for how much care she'd need after the baby came.

Key takeaways

  • When we asked birth mothers what they wished someone had told their partner, most of the answers were about the months before labor.
  • Moms wanted their husbands to learn what they'd need ahead of time, because during contractions many of them could barely talk.
  • Several moms wished their partner had been ready for labor to run long, or to turn complicated and scary.
  • Moms wanted their husband's doubts said out loud and taken to the midwife, not kept to himself.
  • One mom wished her midwife had prepared her husband for how much care a woman needs postpartum.

When I sent out questionnaires for the book, one of the questions for birth mothers was simple: what do you wish someone had told your partner?

I expected answers about the birth itself. Some were. But most of the moms wrote about the months before labor, the stretch where a lot of us husbands figure we're on standby until the big night.

If you're expecting, this is the list your wife might not hand you. These are their words, not mine.

What do moms most wish their husbands knew?

That the birth starts long before labor does. The moms weren't asking for a husband who knows medical terms. They wanted one who shows up early, learns what she'll need, and isn't surprised when labor goes its own way.

One mom put it in a single line. The thing she wished someone had told her husband was "To anticipate needs I didn't know how to express."

That's a big ask, and it's fair. You can't read her mind in the middle of a contraction. You can do most of the learning now, while she can still talk you through it.

Why should he learn what she needs before labor?

Because in labor she may not be able to tell you. Kaelah, a mom from our survey, said that during contractions "it was really hard for me to talk, especially towards the end because I was so tired."

"But this was something that I didn't communicate clearly with him."

Kaelah, birth mother, on wanting more physical touch during contractions

The thing she wanted was simple: more touch. Neither of them had talked about it before labor, so it didn't happen. That's a five-minute conversation over dinner, weeks ahead. Ask her where she wants your hands, what she wants to hear, and what she doesn't want at all. Then write it down. I walk through the rest of that list in what goes in a home birth plan, and what's the partner's part.

Do moms want their husbands at the prenatal appointments?

Yes, and the moms who went to good ones could tell the difference. Lauralee, a mom of five, remembered a hospital practice where she waited and then got a few minutes with the doctor, who asked if she had questions. "I had no idea what to ask," she said.

With her midwife it was different. "Every appointment felt like I was taking a masterclass in women's anatomy and childbirth."

Picture yourself in that room for the masterclass. At our first two births, in the hospital, nobody told me what my role was, and I didn't know enough to ask. The appointments are where you learn enough to ask. Here's more on whether the partner should go to prenatal appointments, and what to do there.

What do moms wish he knew about labor itself?

That it can be long, and it can get hard. Kaelah's answer was short: "It will likely be long! Be prepared."

Another mom said the thing she wished someone had told her husband was "that it could get very complicated and scary." She didn't want him scared during pregnancy. She wanted him ready, so he could stand next to her if it went that way.

Kaelah's labor did change course, and she ended up transferring to the hospital. She told me she hadn't looked into hospitals at all beforehand. "I definitely wish I would have been more prepared and planned for the worst case scenario so that I would have known what I was going into."

That part is yours to own. Ask the midwife which hospital she'd use, learn the route, and know who calls whom. You hope you never need it. I wrote about what happens if a home birth transfers to the hospital if you want the details.

Should he tell her his doubts during pregnancy?

Yes. The moms didn't want a husband who smiles and nods while he quietly worries. Kelly, a mom who once brought the idea of home birth to a hesitant partner, said to listen to his concerns, because they "might raise a good point that you can talk to your midwife or doula about together."

She went further: "They have a say in the matter as well, and their comfort in the birth environment is important."

So say it. Say it to her first, then take it to the midwife together. A doubt that gets an answer in month six is a lot better than one that shows up in your face at hour ten. If you're still at the start of that conversation, read my wife wants a home birth and I'm not sure.

What do moms wish he knew about after the birth?

How much care she'll need, and for how long. When I asked moms what they wished their midwife had done differently to prepare their partner, one answered: "Preparing him on how much care a women needs postpartum."

Most of us plan for the birth and stop there. Plan the weeks after too: who cooks, who does nights, who keeps visitors short. The fourth trimester post covers the partner's jobs once the midwife goes home.

What can he do this week?

Ask her one question and write down the answer: "What will you need from me that you might not be able to say?"

Then put the next prenatal visit on your calendar, and go. Those two things cover more of this list than any book will, mine included.

Frequently Asked Questions

What do moms want from their husbands during pregnancy?

In our survey, moms wanted husbands who came to the prenatal visits, learned ahead of time what they'd need in labor, said their doubts out loud, and planned for the weeks after the birth.

How can a husband prepare for labor during pregnancy?

Ask your wife what she wants from you in labor, including touch and words, and write it down. Go to the midwife visits, learn the hospital transfer plan, and plan who helps in the weeks after.

Should my husband come to my prenatal appointments?

If he can, yes. The appointments are where he gets to know the midwife, asks his own questions, and learns enough about labor to be useful in the room.
