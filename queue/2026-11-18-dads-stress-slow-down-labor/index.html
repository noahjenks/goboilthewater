Can a Dad's Stress Slow Down Labor?

By Noah Jenks, author of Go Boil the Water  ·  November 18, 2026  ·  7 min read

Quick answer

Some birth workers believe it can. Mahika, a birth doula, says she has watched labor pick up many times after a stressed dad left the room, and she is clear that this is a doula's observation, not a research finding. Her answer is to get dads ready so they can stay: trust the midwife and doula to do their jobs, take care of yourself so you stay grounded, and do the practical jobs when the team asks.

Key takeaways

  • Mahika has seen labor speed up after a stressed dad stepped out of the room many times, and she calls it a doula observation rather than science.
  • She doesn't blame the dad, because watching the person you love most in that much pain is frightening for anyone.
  • Her main instruction for fathers is to stop trying to be the midwife or the doula, trust the team, and stay present.
  • If a dad needs to breathe, eat or nap to get back to center, she wants him to do it and come back steady.
  • When a husband is scared of home birth, she starts with the practical plan: what the midwife is trained for, the backup hospital, and a car with a full tank.

Mahika has been a doula for five years. Before that she was a first-time mom in Toronto, where, as she puts it, home birth midwives are "not just legal, it's supported," and she once heard a radio ad promoting home birth while she was driving. She told me she never carried the fear of birth that so many of us pick up somewhere along the way. Her mother and grandmother gave birth in hospitals, so it didn't come from family. It was just never there.

I wanted to talk to her because she works with a lot of dads, and she says something most people in the birth world only hint at. Our stress shows up in the room, and it matters. Here's our full conversation, and below it, what stuck with me.

What has Mahika seen happen when a dad leaves the room?

She has seen labor pick up. More than once, she says, a woman's labor started moving faster soon after her husband stepped out.

"It's not because the dad was there or he was doing something wrong," Mahika told me. "He was stressing out, and he was bringing into her field wrong chemistry so that her birth would start stalling."

She is careful about how far she takes that. "Of course, it's not a scientific observation. This is just a doula observation." Nobody has handed her a study, and she doesn't pretend otherwise. What she has is years of standing in birth rooms and noticing when the mood shifts. She also pointed out that hospital staff rarely think about the partner's state of mind at all. "It's a factor," she said. "It is a factor."

I believe her, because I've felt it from the other side. At our two hospital births I mostly tried to stay upright and out of the way, and my nerves were not a secret to anybody in that room. I wrote more about that in How Do I Stay Calm When I Feel Helpless During Labor?

So should the dad just leave?

No. Mahika's point is that dads need to be ready, and her whole approach is built around getting them there. "That's why it's so important to get dads ready to be at a birth," she said.

She also has no interest in shaming the guy who gets rattled. No amount of reading prepares you for what it feels like, she said, because you're watching the person you love most in real pain. "Of course it's stressful," she said. "It's so understandable, no blame for that."

I asked her if that's where the old line about sending men to boil the water comes from. She thinks so. Mothers and grandmothers stood in that room for generations, and they knew a nervous husband needed something to do with his hands.

What does a doula want the dad to do during labor?

Be grounded and present, and let the team do their jobs. Mahika explains it with a picture from the Vedic tradition. Shakti, the feminine energy, dances. She can be loud and chaotic and move all over the place. Shiva, the masculine, sits still on the mountain. A woman in labor needs to be free to be Shakti, Mahika says, and she can only feel safe being that free if there's a steady Shiva in the room.

"Please do not try to be a midwife. Do not try to be a doula. Do not doula around. This is not what we're asking you for. Have a trust in your team and be present."

Mahika, birth doula

That one hit me, because my instinct as a man is to step in and take over. I've done it in plenty of situations, and it doesn't help. Mahika gives dads permission to do the opposite, and she means it practically. "If you need to self-regulate, if you need to breathe, if you need to nap, if you need to eat, please take care of yourself so that you come back to that center," she said, "and go boil the water when we ask you to."

Yes, she said go boil the water. I didn't put her up to it. Our midwife Gloria really did send me to boil water at our home birth, because the tub wasn't filling fast enough, and that's where the book's title comes from.

What is the difference between a midwife and a doula?

A midwife handles the medical side of the birth, and a doula handles everything else. Mahika gets this question mostly from dads, who ask why they'd need a doula when they already have a midwife.

Her answer is simple. The midwife is a trained medical professional who monitors safety, works to prevent problems and handles emergencies. The doula leaves all of that to the midwife and supports the mother emotionally, spiritually and physically. Mahika says every doula carries "a big bag of treasures," like a rebozo, coaching or hypnobirthing, and uses them only when they'll help. Supporting the father is part of the job too.

There's research behind having someone there just for support. A 2017 Cochrane review of continuous support during childbirth found that women who had continuous support in labor were more likely to have a spontaneous vaginal birth and less likely to have a caesarean, with support from someone like a doula appearing to help most. The authors rated much of the evidence as low quality, so read it as a likely benefit, not a promise. For the full breakdown of roles, see What's the Difference Between a Midwife and a Doula?

Why does Mahika call birth "a birth of three"?

Because a baby isn't the only one being born. "Not only a baby is being born, a mother is being born," she said, even if it's her second or third child. "And then also a father is being born."

She says dads are usually honored when they hear that, and I get why. Historically we've been bystanders at birth. Hearing a doula say the father is being born too puts you inside the event instead of in the corner of it.

What does she tell a husband who is scared of home birth?

She starts with the practical plan, because she's found that's how most men think. She explains what the midwife is trained to watch for, how she prevents and handles problems, and what happens if things don't go the way everyone planned.

Then she gives him his jobs. Park the car nearby, keep the tank full, know the address of the nearest hospital and have the documents ready. "This is the psychology of a man who boils the water," she said. Once those questions are answered, she says, a lot of dads let out a sigh of relief, because they can see that home birth with a midwife is an organized plan with a backup.

Only after that does she move on to hormones and how safe a woman feels in her own home, and then to birth as a spiritual event, which she says many fathers are hearing about for the first time. If you want the evidence on safety, including where the medical groups disagree, start with Is Home Birth Safe? What Partners Should Know. If the backup plan is what keeps you up at night, read What Happens If We Need to Transfer to the Hospital During a Home Birth?

Staying steady when you feel useless is the whole subject of Chapter 8 of the book, and Mahika put it better than I did. You can find her on Instagram at @gentle.birth.doula, where she shares education on every kind of birth, not only home birth.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a stressed partner slow down labor?

Some doulas and midwives say they've seen it. Mahika, a birth doula, has watched labor pick up after a stressed dad left the room many times, though she calls that a doula's observation, not a research finding. Her fix is preparing dads ahead of time so they can stay calm and stay in the room.

What should a dad do if he feels overwhelmed during labor?

Take care of yourself so you can come back steady. Mahika tells fathers to breathe, eat or even nap if they need to, trust the midwife and doula to do their jobs, and take on practical tasks when the team asks.

Does a home birth still need a backup plan?

Yes. Mahika walks scared dads through it first: what the midwife is trained to watch for and handle, the address of the nearest hospital, a car parked close with a full tank, and the documents ready to go.
