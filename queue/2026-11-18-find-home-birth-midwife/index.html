How Do I Find a Home Birth Midwife Near Me?

By Noah Jenks, author of Go Boil the Water  ·  November 18, 2026  ·  6 min read

Quick answer

Start with people who have had a home birth near you, since most of the couples in our surveys found their midwife through a referral from a friend or family member. A local doula, a home birth group, or an online search can fill in the gaps. Then meet two or three midwives together and ask each one which credential she holds and whether she's licensed where you live, because the rules differ by state and country.

Key takeaways

  • A local doula often knows the home birth midwives in her area, and one dad in our survey met his midwife through their doula.

When Nick Lampp's midwife came to the house for a prenatal visit, she'd have a cup of tea first. She'd ask how life was going, and only then ask his wife to lie on the couch for a quick check. "It was just like a friend visiting," Nick told me.

That's the kind of midwife you're looking for: someone your wife trusts and you'd be glad to have in your house at 2 a.m. The trouble is that when you start looking, you may not know a single home birth midwife's name. Here's where couples actually find them, and what your part in the search looks like.

Where do most couples find a home birth midwife?

Through people they know. When we surveyed parents for the book, we asked how they chose their midwife. Four of the six dads answered "Referral from friend or family," and so did most of the moms.

Sometimes the referral is very close to home. Nathan, a dad from our survey, said their midwife "is close with my family because they delivered two of my sisters' babies." Gabriel's wife got the idea for a home birth from her sister, who'd had her own kids at home. Asked what prepared him most, Gabriel said, "Conversations with someone who had experience."

So start talking. Ask friends who've had a homebirth who their midwife was and whether they'd hire her again. Ask what she was like at 3 a.m., and how she treated the dad. People who had a good home birth usually love talking about their midwife.

Can a doula help us find a midwife?

Often, yes. A doula who works in your area has usually been to births with the local midwives, so she's a good person to ask who practices near you. Sean, a dad from our survey, said his wife decided on a home birth after hearing about friends who were planning theirs, "and thru our doula we met an amazing midwife."

Just keep the two jobs straight. Mahika, an international doula I talked with, put it simply: "Midwife is a medical professional who is trained to handle emergencies and to monitor medical safety of a birth." The doula, she said, does something else. "She leaves all of that for the midwife to handle." If you're still fuzzy on who does what, here's the difference between a midwife and a doula.

Do online searches and local birth groups work?

They do, especially if you're new to the area or don't know anyone who's had a home birth. Some of the moms in our survey found their midwife with an online search, and one used a search and a friend's referral together.

Local home birth groups are worth a look too, whether they meet in person or run a page online. They're full of parents who just went through this. Post the question plainly: who did you use, and would you use her again? Then treat whatever names you collect as a starting list. The decision comes after you meet them.

Is there a directory of home birth midwives?

We're building one. The Go Boil the Water midwife and doula directory will be a free place for families to find home birth midwives and doulas near them. It isn't open yet. We're collecting listings now and will open it once each region has enough practitioners to be useful to families, so there's nothing to search today.

When it opens, each listing will show the practitioner's credential, where she's based and the areas she serves, whether she attends home births, and her own answer to one question: How do you include partners in your care? Other directories tell you where someone practices. This one will also tell a nervous husband what it's like to work with her.

You can leave your email on that page to hear when it goes live. And if you already have a midwife or doula you love, send her the link. Listings are free. A listing won't be an endorsement, though, and you'll still need to check her credentials yourself.

What credentials should a home birth midwife have?

In the US you'll mostly see three: Certified Nurse-Midwife (CNM), Certified Midwife (CM), and Certified Professional Midwife (CPM). According to the American College of Nurse-Midwives (ACNM), CNMs and CMs are both certified by the American Midwifery Certification Board. A CNM is also a registered nurse with a graduate degree, and a CM comes from a non-nursing background with at least a bachelor's degree. A CPM is certified by the North American Registry of Midwives (NARM), and ACNM's comparison says CPMs attend births in homes and birth centers rather than hospitals.

The rules about who can attend a home birth differ by state and by country, and I'm not going to pretend to know yours. Ask each midwife which credential she holds, whether she's licensed where you live, and how she works with local doctors and hospitals. A good midwife will answer all of it plainly. The full set of interview questions, from safety to transfers to your own role, is in How Do I Interview a Home Birth Midwife, and What Should I Ask?

What's the partner's job in finding a midwife?

Take some of the work off her plate. In a lot of homes, the mom does all the research and the dad turns up for the birth. You can change that in the first week of looking.

Book the calls. Once you have two or three names, offer to email or phone them and set up the consultations. Put them on both your calendars.

Then come to the consultation. Gloria, our midwife, told me the dads who come to as many appointments as possible "do a thousand percent better," and the first meeting is the first of those. Gabriel said of his wife's midwives, "It was important to them that I was at every meeting. I liked that." Their practice had several midwives working together, and whoever was available that day came to the birth. "So we had met with them all," he said. If you're interviewing a group practice, ask to meet everyone who might show up. (More on why the visits matter in Should I Go to the Prenatal Appointments?)

And ask your own questions. Write down your worries before you go and say them out loud, even the ones that feel rude. Nathan went in worried about the midwife's training. Afterward he said, "clearly I underestimated their knowledge and experience." You only get to find that out if you ask.

What does it feel like to meet the midwife for the first time?

For a lot of dads, it's where the fear starts to loosen. Denny went in hesitant. Home birth, he said, "was not something I ever wanted nor thought was super safe." He studied and talked to people who had done it, and then he met the midwife. "The first meeting I had with our midwife was so important," he said.

It was the same for me. My own resistance to home birth started to give way in a midwife consultation. It was hard to keep arguing with someone who had clearly attended more births than I could count and answered everything without blinking.

"I wanted to get pregnant again right away because I missed hanging out with our midwife and the team."

Denny, dad

Nick's full story is in my conversation with Nick. On the drive home from each consultation, ask your wife how she felt, and tell her how you felt. If either of you can't picture this woman in your house during labor, keep looking.

What if there aren't many home birth midwives near us?

Ask how far they travel. Nick's midwife lived over an hour away. When labor started early in the morning, she said she'd have a shower and then head down, and they also lived in a city, close to a hospital. Distance worked for them because the plan was clear.

So ask every midwife how far she'll come, how long it usually takes her to reach you, and what she wants you to do if labor moves faster than her drive. Then ask your midwife to walk you both through the hospital plan for your address.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I find a home birth midwife near me?

Ask friends and family who have had a home birth, ask a local doula or home birth group, and try an online search. Then meet two or three midwives together and ask each one about her credentials and her license where you live.

What's the difference between a CNM and a CPM?

According to the American College of Nurse-Midwives, a Certified Nurse-Midwife (CNM) is a registered nurse with a graduate degree, certified by the American Midwifery Certification Board. A Certified Professional Midwife (CPM) is certified by the North American Registry of Midwives and attends births in homes and birth centers. The rules for each differ by state and country, so ask your midwife.

Should the partner go to the first midwife consultation?

Yes. The partner will be in the room with the midwife during labor, so he needs to trust her too. Go together, bring your own questions, and talk about how it felt on the way home.

Does Go Boil the Water have a midwife directory?

It's being built. The free Go Boil the Water directory of home birth midwives and doulas is collecting listings now and will open once each region has enough practitioners. Each listing will include how that midwife or doula includes partners in her care.
