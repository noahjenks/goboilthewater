What Should the Partner Do in the Last Weeks Before a Home Birth?

By Noah Jenks, author of Go Boil the Water  ·  December 4, 2026  ·  5 min read

Quick answer

In the last weeks before a home birth, the partner's job is to have everything ready so she doesn't have to think about it. Set up the birth space and test the supplies, learn who's on call and how to reach them, know the hospital Plan B, keep your phone on and stay close to home, and take over the house so she can rest.

Key takeaways

  • In the final weeks, the partner's main job is to have the practical things done so she can rest.
  • Do a test run of the birth space now, including whether your water heater can fill the pool.
  • Know who's on call, how to reach the midwife, and when she wants to hear from you.
  • Plan B should be settled before labor: which hospital, the route, the bag, and who calls whom.
  • Stay reachable and close to home, and take on the house, the meals and the family updates.

If she's thirty-six weeks along and you're just now getting serious, you're not too late. You do need to spend the time well.

Here's how I think about these weeks. The birth is going to ask her for everything. Your job right now is to take as much as you can off her plate before it starts, so when labor comes, the only thing she has to do is labor.

What is the partner's job in the last weeks of pregnancy?

To get the practical things done so she can rest. The house, the supplies, the plan, the phone list. She's tired, she's carrying a baby, and she's probably been the one doing most of the planning so far. Rowan, a dad I interviewed, said it honestly: "I feel like I let Genine run the show, and that's to be understood. But I could've contributed more in the pre-birth phase."

These weeks are your chance to contribute more.

How do we get the house ready?

Do a full test run now. Set up the room where she wants to labor, unpack the supplies the midwife asked for, and put them where you can find them in the dark.

If she's planning a water birth, test-fill the pool. The night our daughter Amani was born, our hot water heater had been drained and we couldn't fill the birth tub fast enough. Our midwife, Gloria, sent me to boil water on the stove, which is where the book got its name. It's a good story now. You'd rather have a full tub. I go through the whole setup in how to get your house ready for a home birth.

Who's on call, and when do we call them?

Learn the phone list cold. The midwife, her backup, the doula if you have one, whoever is watching the kids. Save the numbers in your phone and write them on paper by the door.

Ask the midwife at your next visit when she wants to hear from you once labor starts, and how. Don't guess, and don't wait for labor to find out. She'll tell you what she wants to know and when. Write down her answer. If you're fuzzy on the early stretch, read what the partner does during early labor at home.

Is Plan B ready?

It should be, before labor. Which hospital, the route, how long the drive takes at 3 a.m., what goes in the bag, who calls whom. Kaelah, a mom from our survey who transferred, said she wished she had "planned for the worst case scenario so that I would have known what I was going into."

Keep the car fueled. Keep her documents in the bag. Mahika, an international doula I interviewed, told me "the men's psychology works in a very practical way." She said the man who boils the water "will have a car nearby, have his tank full, have the address of the hospital nearby. He will prepare the documents." There's more in what happens if you transfer to the hospital.

Should I stay close to home?

Yes, as much as you can. Keep your phone on and charged, with the ringer up. If you travel for work, talk with your boss now about what happens when the call comes. If you're more than an hour away on a normal day, have a plan for how you get home fast, and who sits with her until you do.

Labor doesn't always give much warning. Melissa, a mom of four, told me that during her fourth labor things moved so quickly that "within about 15-30 minutes, our baby was crowning." That was her fourth baby, and every labor is different. The point is to be reachable if yours moves fast.

How do I help her rest?

Take the house. Cook, or line up meals for the freezer. Do the laundry, the dishes, the school runs. Handle the texts from family who keep asking "anything yet?" so she doesn't have to answer them.

One mom told me the thing she wished someone had told her husband was "To anticipate needs I didn't know how to express." In these weeks that mostly means noticing what she's still carrying, and picking it up without being asked.

What if we haven't talked about the birth much?

Start now, one conversation at a time. Ask what she wants from you in labor: where she wants your hands, what she wants to hear, what she doesn't want at all. Ask her birth rules. Write it down. I listed the talks I'd have in what a couple should talk about before a home birth.

Frequently Asked Questions

What should a husband do at 36 weeks pregnant?

Get the birth space and supplies ready, learn the on-call list and when the midwife wants to hear from you, settle the hospital Plan B, stay reachable, and take over the house so she can rest.

Should the partner stop traveling before the due date?

Many partners stay close to home in the last weeks. If you can't, talk with your employer early and have a plan for getting home fast and for who stays with her until you arrive.

When should we call the midwife once labor starts?

Ask your midwife ahead of time. She'll tell you when she wants to hear from you and how. Write her answer down and keep it with the phone list.
